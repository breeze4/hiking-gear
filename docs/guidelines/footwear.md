# Footwear — Shoes, Socks, Gaiters

Footwear is the single most consequential and most personal gear decision. Fit comes first; everything else is secondary. Sources: Skurka's footwear chapter, Clelland's *Ultralight Backpackin' Tips* 79–89, Clelland & Ladigin *Lighten Up!* pp. 49–53.

**Weight-on-feet math:** Legg & Mahanty (1986) — moving weight on the feet costs ~6× the energy of moving the same weight in the pack. Dropping a pair of shoes from 4 lb to 2 lb is equivalent to taking ~12 lb out of the pack. This is the load-bearing reason every source pushes trail runners over boots.

---

## Shoes

**Principles**

- Default to low-cut, non-waterproof trail-running shoes for 3-season. Mesh uppers breathe and dry fast; less material absorbs less water; ~1.5–2 lb/pair vs. 3–4 lb for a boot; no break-in soreness. Shorter lifespan (~500 mi vs. 1000+) is the tradeoff.
- Waterproof uppers are a net loss for 3-season. WP/B seams fail with dirt, sweat, and abrasion; leather sealant wears off; and the shoe has a big opening at the ankle that water pours into. Wet feet are managed by sock rotation, not by trying to keep them dry.
- High-top ankle support is overrated for light loads with trekking poles. Weak ankles still roll; high tops push unevenness up to knees and hips. But heavy packs, weak ankles, or rugged off-trail are a legitimate reason to escalate — in that order, try a hiking shoe, then a lightweight hiking boot.
- Reject sandals-as-primary and minimalist/barefoot shoes for backpacking. Upper protection and underfoot protection matter.
- **Fit is non-negotiable.** Feature buzzwords (wide toe box, zero drop, rocker, max cushion) are only means to fit. Match length, width, volume, and foot shape; secure the heel; allow a natural gait.
- Size up ~½ size from street shoes to accommodate foot swelling from heat, altitude, and miles.
- Replace when you can feel sharp rocks under the instep or balls of the feet — the cushioning is gone.

**Fit procedure**

1. Shop late afternoon; feet are swollen then.
2. Wear the exact sock you'll hike in. The larger foot gets fit.
3. Unlaced, toes jammed forward: one to two fingers of space between heel and heel cup (Skurka says one; Ladigin says two, because wrinkles "shorten" shoes with wear).
4. Laced: heel doesn't slide up/down or fore-aft; toes wiggle freely but aren't sloppy side-to-side; smallest toe isn't squeezed.
5. Walk an incline and firmly kick the toe forward. Toes must not jam the front.
6. Confirm a natural foot-plant. If your gait changes, the shoe is wrong.

**Picks**

- *La Sportiva Ultra Raptor* — Skurka's 3-season off-trail-capable default. Vibram sole, sturdy upper. ~12 oz.
- *Adidas Terrex Fast R* or *Salewa Ultra Train* — Skurka's other off-trail-capable picks at similar weight.
- *Altra Lone Peak* — cushioned easy-trail pick; wide toe box; common template default. Lower protection for talus than the Sportivas.
- *Salomon X Ultra* or *Merrell Capra Sport* — "more shoe" options for heavier packs or hikers wanting more underfoot and upper protection short of a boot.

Skurka gives no insole endorsement — stock inserts are "sock liners" but aftermarket Superfeet/SOLE/Spenco help specific biomechanical cases (leg-length discrepancy, high arches, very wide or narrow feet). If you use them, bring them to the fitting.

## Gaiters

**Principles**

- Optional. Most useful with shorts, in dusty/sandy trails, or any terrain that chucks pebbles and schmutz into the ankle opening. Less critical with pants.
- Want: light, stretchy, breathable, ankle-height. Reject WP/B mountaineering-style gaiters — poor breathability, coarse S/M-L/XL sizing, and the instep strap wears through fast because trail runners don't have an instep to hold it.
- Template weight cap: 4 oz/pair. Real-world picks weigh 1–2 oz.

**Picks**

- *Simblissity LevaGaiter* (~2 oz/pair) — Skurka's long-term pick. No instep strap, no cord, stays put. Often unavailable; Simblissity is a hobby operation with no direct equivalent.
- *Dirty Girl Gaiters* (~1.2 oz/pair) — fine for on-trail; too flimsy for rugged off-trail.

## Hiking socks A & B

**Principles**

- One sock, not a liner + sock stack. Extra layers add friction protection at the cost of heat and moisture — and heat + moisture are two of the three blister factors. Want more padding? Use a more cushioned shoe, not a thicker sock.
- **Materials:** merino wool or synthetic (polyester) main fiber, nylon reinforcement at toe and heel, a little spandex for fit. Never cotton. Pure wool blows out at the Achilles fast.
- **Fit:** too big bunches and blisters; too tight or a tight cuff chokes circulation (worse in cold, worse again for anyone with circulatory issues).
- **Two pairs for 3-season dry-climate rotation.** Both pairs are the same model — A and B are interchangeable. The rotation exists so a washed pair has all day to dry.

**Dry-climate rotation protocol (Skurka)**

1. Leave camp wearing A. B lives in the pack.
2. Mid-day, swap. Hand-wash A in water (no soap), clip it to the pack compression straps to dry as you walk.
3. Arrive in camp in B. Air feet. Put on clean-dry A for the evening.
4. Leave camp in A next morning. Wash B mid-day. Repeat.

Lighten Up!'s variant: wash the day's pair at camp in the evening; hike the next day on the other. Same idea, slower cadence.

**Picks**

- *DeFeet Wooleator 3-inch* — Skurka's long-term default. 63/36/1 merino/nylon/spandex, ~2 oz/pair. Merino blend (Wooleator) beats the polyester sibling (Aireator) for odor on multi-day trips. Replace every ~300 trail miles.
- *Darn Tough Run/Light Hiker No Show* — common template pick; lifetime warranty.
- *Injinji Trail Midweight* — toe socks for hikers who get between-toe blisters. Not a universal pick; it's a fix for a specific problem.
- Other reputable specialists: SmartWool, Wigwam.

## Camp footwear

**Principles**

- Usually unnecessary. Well-tested primary shoes don't need a backup, and a second pair of shoes in prolonged wet just means two soaked pairs.
- Bring dedicated camp footwear only when you expect lots of camp time *and* wet hiking shoes. Even then, the lightest solution is free: **plastic bread bags or gallon freezer bags over dry camp socks, worn inside the wet shoes.** Keeps the dry socks dry, durable enough for a few miles of camp puttering.
- End-of-day foot relief without camp shoes: loosen laces, pull the insole, soak feet in a cold stream.

**Picks**

- *Bread bags / gallon freezer bags* — Skurka and Clelland both independently endorse. Essentially weightless.
- If you insist on a real shoe: airline slippers, *Crocs*, or clog-style sandals with heel straps. Light enough to justify only in the camp-time-plus-wet-shoes case above.

## Sleeping socks

**Principles**

- A dedicated dry pair, worn only in camp and in the sleeping bag. Never worn during the day in normal conditions. In wet trips this sock is critical — sleeping in damp socks risks trench foot, which can start from above-freezing wet exposure sustained 12+ hours.
- Slightly thicker than the daytime sock, slightly roomier (no constriction). Same material rules: merino or synthetic blend, nylon reinforcement, small spandex.
- Store them inside the sleeping bag so they can't get lost or damp in the tent.
- The bedtime routine matters as much as the sock: pull off daytime socks, inspect and clean feet, tape any hot spots with Leukotape, then put on the sleeping pair.

**Picks**

- *DeFeet Woolie Boolie 6-inch* — Skurka's escalation pick; Wooleator fabric, thicker. ~3 oz/pair. Can double as a daytime sock in genuinely cold conditions.
- *Darn Tough* midweight crew — common template default for a dry sleeping pair.
- Any dry pair of the hiking-sock model you trust also works — the job is "clean, dry, warm."

## Foot care adjacent to the sock system

These belong in the first-aid kit but affect sock/shoe choice:

- **Leukotape P** (zinc oxide, rayon-backed) for hot-spot prevention and active blister taping. Moleskin is boot-era gear and doesn't stick well to flimsy UL shoes.
- **Hydropel** (petroleum-based ointment) rubbed under the whole foot at night and morning dramatically reduces "cadaver foot" in sustained-wet conditions.

## Deferred to override files

- Sustained-wet terrain (Alaska tundra, SW canyons, early-season Rockies) — neoprene socks, the "daytime pair stays soaked, sleeping pair stays dry" collapse of the rotation. Belongs in a wet-climate override.
- Winter / below-freezing — waterproof backpacking boots, vapor-barrier socks, neoprene overboots, mukluks. Out of 3-season scope.
- Sandals-as-primary, barefoot shoes — sources document them but reject them; skip unless a specific trip calls for it.
