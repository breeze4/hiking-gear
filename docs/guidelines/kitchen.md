# Kitchen — Stove, Pot, Fuel, Utensil, Ignition

The cook system. Source: Skurka "Cooking systems" chapter; Clelland Tips 118–128, 144, 146; *Lighten Up!* kitchen chapter.

**Core principle: a cook system is a system, not a stove.** At minimum: heat source + cookware + ignition + utensil, plus (optionally) a mug and extra eating containers for groups. Pick based on group size, meal type, frequency of cooking, fuel availability, and local regulations — weight and boil time are secondary.

**Core 3-season default:** alcohol (or Esbit) stove + wide-short titanium/aluminum pot + windscreen (Caldera Cone or foil) + Bic mini + long plastic spoon + plastic-bottle fuel container. Total weight ~8–10 oz. All three sources converge here for solo and two-person trips.

**Boil water, don't cook.** Most backpacking meals reconstitute in hot water inside the pot or a freezer bag (instant potatoes, couscous, freezer-bag meals, ramen). This frees you from flame control and turns the stove into a water heater.

## Stove (heat source)

**Principles**

- **Alcohol stove** is the default for solo and two-person 3-season trips. Tiny weight, fuel is cheap and legal to fly/mail, works in any plastic bottle, no moving parts to break. Downsides: no flame control, slow boil (~6–8 min), flame nearly invisible in daylight, inappropriate for groups of 3+ or snow-melting.
- **Esbit (solid-fuel)** is the alcohol alternative for hikers who want zero setup complexity. Half-ounce cubes boil ~2 cups in 7 min. Fuel legal to fly/mail. Downsides: ~5× alcohol's fuel cost, sticky residue on pot bottom, funny smell (triple-bag it).
- **Canister stove** is the right answer for groups of 3+, small high-use groups, trips where you cook many meals per day, and winter. Fast boil, fast setup, flame control, excellent ease of use. Downsides: fuel legal only at outdoor retailers (not mailable, not flyable), small canisters are ~half packaging weight, not user-fillable so you always carry more than needed, canisters must be trashed or recycled.
- **Liquid-fuel** (white gas) is winter expedition gear. Heavy, temperamental, fire-hazard priming, noxious fumes. Wrong choice for any 3-season backpacking trip.
- **Wood stove** (Solo Stove, BushBuddy) has zero fuel weight and is compelling only on very long unsupported trips where you can't resupply alcohol. Downsides: soot, smoke, prohibited during fire danger, pre-collect dry tinder before storms.
- **Open fire cooking** is wonderful ambience and practical almost never. Hard to start in wind or wet, slow, sooty, often prohibited. Not a primary strategy.
- **Going stoveless** is a legitimate choice for short trips (2–4 days). Heavier food but no stove/fuel/pot weight. Good for stormy arrivals when lighting a stove is miserable.
- **Windscreen is mandatory** for alcohol and Esbit. DIY from aluminum foil is fine; a Trail Designs Caldera Cone or Sidewinder doubles as windscreen and pot stand, and improves stability. A proper windscreen easily offsets its own weight in fuel savings.
- **Safety rules for alcohol:** never add liquid fuel to a lit stove (the reservoir flares); let it burn out rather than trying to "shut it off"; check the flame is out by holding a hand well above the stove (it's often invisible in daylight); light with the index finger (not thumb) and an "elegant artist" hand shape so a flare doesn't catch your palm.
- **Leave-no-trace:** alcohol flames scorch ground. Cook on mineral soil, sand, naturally sterile river/alpine surfaces, or pre-wet grass. **Never cook directly on flat sandstone in the desert Southwest** — the scar lasts decades.

**Picks**

- *Trail Designs Caldera Cone / Sidewinder Ti-Tri* — Skurka's pick. Alcohol stove + pot-sized titanium cone that's both windscreen and stand. $60–100, highly efficient, durable.
- *Trail Designs Kojin / Zelph Star-Lyte* — cottage alcohol burners, ~0.5 oz.
- *Trangia* (3.2 oz brass) — Clelland's pick when 3+ hikers share a stove. Swedish, made since 1925, bulletproof.
- *Fancy Feast / Super Cat DIY* — cheapest alcohol stove; punch holes in a cat-food can with a paper punch. Pot sits directly on the can (no stand).
- *Esbit Pocket Stove* — $15, ~3.25 oz closed, holds 6 cubes inside. Lightweight solid-fuel option.
- *Snow Peak LiteMax* — lightest canister upright stove, ~2 oz.
- *MSR PocketRocket 2* / *Soto Amicus* — mainstream canister picks.
- *Jetboil Flash* — integrated canister system; only pays off for heavy cookers and groups.
- *Kovea Spider* — remote canister with liquid feed, winter-capable.

## Wind screen

**Principles**

- Required for alcohol and Esbit; strongly improves canister stove efficiency too. Skip only for integrated canister systems with built-in wind protection (Jetboil).
- DIY heavy-duty aluminum foil or tooling foil is functional and free. Roll up for packing.
- Caldera-style cones are the commercial sweet spot — windscreen + stand + stability, pot-specific fit.
- Leave a gap at the bottom of the windscreen for oxygen, and keep the screen ~1/2" off the pot so the stove can still draw.

**Picks**

- *Trail Designs Caldera Cone / Sidewinder* — pot-specific titanium cone.
- *MSR WindBurner* foil — commercial alternative for canister stoves.
- *DIY aluminum foil* — free, works, replace when it gets shredded.

## Pot stand

**Principles**

- Needed only when the stove doesn't support the pot itself. A DIY Fancy Feast or Super Cat sits the pot directly on the can — no stand. Larger cans, Trangias, and remote burners need one.
- **Three tent stakes** form a perfectly stable platform — classic multiuse move, zero extra weight.
- Commercial pot stands are usually bundled with the stove or windscreen system.

**Picks**

- *Three tent stakes* — free, already in the kit.
- *Trail Designs Sidewinder* — integrates stand with windscreen.
- *Ti-Tri* from Trail Designs — same idea, different packing profile.

## Cookpot

**Principles**

- **Volume target:** 475–950 mL per person for 3-season. The top of the range is the "don't be stupid light" pot — an extra 100 mL costs almost nothing in weight and pays off every meal. Skurka's explicit note: "the weight difference between a 16-ounce and 32-ounce pot is minor, but the convenience factor is huge."
- Clelland's more aggressive minimums: 500 mL cup solo, 1 L pot for two, 1.3 L for three.
- Real-world sweet spot: **750–900 mL solo, 1.3–1.5 L for two.**
- **Shape: short and wide beats tall and narrow** at the same volume. More stable, more fuel-efficient (flames wrap the base, not the sidewalls), easier to clean.
- **Cover the pot when heating.** An aluminum lid or foil cap cuts boil time and fuel use dramatically. A tight lid is critical for "cozy cooking" where the meal finishes off-stove.
- **Material:**
  - *Aluminum* — excellent thermal conductor, cooks evenly, cheap, heavier than titanium. Don't cook acidic foods (tomato) in bare aluminum.
  - *Hard-anodized aluminum* — coating harder than stainless; the best pick for users who actually cook rather than just boil.
  - *Titanium* — lightest, most durable, pricey. Poor thermal conductivity matters less at thin wall thicknesses; windscreen and pot shape matter more than material for efficiency. Scorches food easily if you try to actually cook — keep stirring or just boil.
  - *Stainless steel* — cheap, heavy, poor conductor. Car-camping grade.
- **Eating from the pot** is standard for soloists — no separate bowl needed. In 2-person groups, bring one extra eating container so the second person doesn't wait for the pot.

**Picks**

- *Evernew Titanium Ultralight 900 mL* — Skurka's "Cadillac" pot. The template default. Light, efficient, fits a gas canister inside.
- *Toaks Titanium 750 mL / 900 mL* — budget titanium alternative, same spec.
- *Stanco Grease Pot* — $10, ~4 oz aluminum pot. Skurka's budget pick; "best balance of weight and value" for average backpackers.
- *Vargo Titanium BOT* — bottle-shaped pot for alcohol users who want a leak-tight transport option.
- *MSR Titan Kettle* — common single-person boil-only pick.
- *Snow Peak Trek 1400* — bigger two-person pot.

## Eating bowl or pot

**Principles**

- **Soloist: eat from the cook pot.** No separate container needed. Clean with the spoon and a finger of water.
- **2-person group:** one extra eating container (the person with the container gets first dibs; the other eats from the pot once it's freed up). Plus two mugs and two spoons.
- **3+ group:** each person needs an eating container, utensil, and (optionally) a mug. Metal containers are best — they can be re-warmed on the stove if a meal cools before everyone's served.
- **Clelland's oily-ingredients trick:** put sauces, cheese, and oil in personal mugs rather than the shared pot. Cleanup is dramatically easier because the pot stays dry.

**Picks**

- *Ziploc freezer bag* — zero-weight eating container for freezer-bag cooking; pour hot water in, seal, wait 10 min, eat with a spoon. Only the spoon needs cleaning.
- *GSI Fairshare mug* — lightweight plastic bowl/mug hybrid, common group pick.
- *Snow Peak Titanium Bowl* — for groups that actually cook rather than reconstitute.

## Hot drink container (mug)

**Principles**

- A dedicated mug is a legitimate weight add even for soloists. It lets you have a hot drink *with* your meal instead of waiting for a second boil cycle. Skurka: "the extra few ounces are well worth the time savings."
- Plastic mugs are under $5 and light. Titanium mugs are similar weight but more durable and far pricier.
- Double-wall insulated mugs keep drinks hot longer but cost more weight; marginal for 3-season.
- **Multi-use:** Clelland carries a small titanium mug even when stoveless, because it's also a cereal bowl and a fire-heating vessel for foot-care water in an emergency.

**Picks**

- *Reusable plastic Starbucks mug* — Skurka's budget pick. ~$2 at a store, ~2 oz.
- *Toaks Titanium 450 mL mug* — template default for long-term users.
- *Snow Peak Titanium 450* — similar spec, slightly pricier.

## Pot lifter

**Principles**

- Only needed if the pot lacks integrated handles or if the stove is tippy (common with DIY alcohol on a foil platform). A small aluminum pot gripper weighs ~1 oz.
- A gloved hand works in a pinch, but: wool gloves toast, synthetic gloves melt.
- If the pot has a stable bail or side handles, skip the lifter.

**Picks**

- *MSR PanHandler / Primus aluminum pot gripper* — ~1 oz.

## Fuel container

**Principles**

- **Alcohol:** any plastic drink bottle. Small (4–8 oz) Gatorade or water bottle, measured to exactly what you need for the trip. Add a squirt cap pilfered from a hot-sauce bottle to prevent pouring drips.
- **Esbit:** original packaging, or a small odor-proof Ziploc / LOKSAK to contain the smell.
- **Canister:** the canister is its own container — no extra needed.
- **Liquid fuel (if you must carry it):** use a bottle with an unmistakably different shape from your water bottles. Drinking white gas in the middle of the night is a known failure mode.

**Picks**

- *Disposable drink bottle* — Skurka's pick. ~1 oz, free, sized per trip.
- *LOKSAK* small — for Esbit cube storage and odor containment.

## Fuel measurement

**Principles**

- Both Skurka and Clelland budget fuel quantitatively rather than eyeballing. An actual measuring tool (plastic medicine cup, marked fuel bottle) lets you carry *only* what the trip needs.
- **Skurka's alcohol budget:** ~0.7 oz per meal with his Super Cat stove. He uses a plastic medicine cup.
- **Clelland's alcohol budget:** 0.06 L (~2 oz) per person per day in warm weather, 0.075 L (~2.5 oz) per person per day in cool weather (Alaskan summer). Includes breakfast and dinner.
- Both are heuristic starting points — tune them to your stove, windscreen, ambient temperature, and how much hot drink you brew.
- Over-carrying fuel "just in case" is a classic beginner move that adds half a pound for no benefit. Measure, don't guess.

**Picks**

- *Plastic medicine cup* (1 oz) — Skurka's pick. Free from most pharmacies.
- *Marked fuel bottle* — write oz lines on the bottle with a Sharpie.

## Utensil

**Principles**

- **One long-handled spoon.** Forks and knives add nothing to a ramen/couscous/oatmeal-based diet. "A spoon that resembles a shovel" (Skurka).
- **Long handle** so it reaches the bottom of freezer bags and deeper pots without dipping knuckles in hot food.
- **Plastic over titanium.** Titanium spoons are expensive and cold to the touch (mouth discomfort in cool weather). Plastic is cheap, warm, functional, replaceable.
- **Avoid sporks.** They hold less liquid than a spoon and the tines snag freezer bags.
- Trim the handle for packability if you cook from a short pot; leave it long for winter snow-melt pots.

**Picks**

- *GSI Outdoors Pouch Spoon* — Skurka's pick. $1, 0.3 oz plastic. Handle trimmed for 3-season, full length for winter.
- *Sea to Summit Alpha Light Long Spoon* — titanium alternative for durability-first users.
- *Humangear GoBites Uno* — durable plastic with a scooped bowl; common template pick.
- *Toaks Titanium Long Handle Spoon* — template default titanium option.

## Ignition

**Principles**

- **Bic mini lighter** is the universal pick. 2" tall, ~0.4 oz, wind-resistant flame, long-lasting, cheap. Choose a bright color (yellow/orange) so it doesn't disappear in camp. Optional: pull the childproof guard with needle-nose pliers so cold hands can strike it.
- **Backup:** a paper book of matches (~0.1 oz) in a tiny plastic bag, or a second mini Bic. Emergency-only — you probably won't use it.
- **Avoid ferro rods and magnesium sparkers for stove ignition.** They need a stable surface for accurate sparks, which most stoves don't provide. Skurka: "better in theory than practice."
- **Even if the stove has an integrated piezo igniter, carry a backup.** Piezos fail.
- **Wet Bic revival:** a soaked Bic works again once dry. Stow head-down in an inside pocket to dry.
- **Stormproof matches are unnecessary** for 3-season backpacking — a Bic does the job fine.

**Picks**

- *Bic Mini* — primary pick.
- *Book of paper matches* in a small Ziploc — emergency backup.
- *UCO Stormproof Match Kit* — overkill for 3-season; legitimate for long winter or expedition use.

## The cozy (Clelland's signature idea)

A formfitting insulating sleeve sized to the pot, made from old closed-cell pad foam or reflective bubble-wrap insulation. Not a template row, but it's load-bearing for the ultralight alcohol kitchen.

**Why it's worth it:**

- Easily offsets its own weight in fuel savings.
- Finishes "cooking" reconstituted meals off-stove while the contents sit in the cozy — the stove only has to bring water to boil.
- Keeps food warm while eating.
- Lets you hold the pot without burning yourself (no pot lifter needed).
- Cushions the pot inside the pack against the back panel.

**Standard cozy-cook method:** boil water, dump the freezer-bag contents into the pot, kill the stove, slip the pot into the cozy, cover, wait ~5 minutes. Add ~1.5 oz of calorie-dense fat (spiced olive oil) at the end. Clelland's book recipes are calibrated to ~4.5 oz dry food and ~300 mL water per person per meal.

DIY from an old foam pad or from Reflectix insulation sold in hardware stores. Sized to the pot with the lid snugly covered.

## Cleanup

**Principles**

- **No soap. No scrubby.** Fingers and water for most meals.
- **Sand and water** for burnt or stuck-on food.
- **Spoon-scrape the pot empty** as you eat so there's almost nothing left to clean.
- **Disperse gray water in a wide arc** well away from water sources — don't concentrate in one spot (draws animals).
- **In bear country or arid zones, drink the gray water** instead of dispersing it.
- **Cook and clean away from camp and the food storage area.** Cooking smells linger and draw wildlife.
