# Worn — Go Suit & Items Always On

The always-on layer: what you hike in, the items that live on your body, and trekking poles. These pieces manage moisture, protect skin from sun/brush/bugs, and assist locomotion. Source: Skurka's "Go Suit" tier (the first of his four clothing tiers), supplemented by Clelland's master gear list and *Lighten Up!*'s clothing chapter.

**Core discipline:** one of each, not two. One hiking shirt, one bottom, one pair of underwear. Wash in water during the day (soap only in town). Redundancy lives in the Stop & Sleep tier, not here.

---

## Hiking shirt

**Principles**

- Synthetic (polyester, nylon) or merino wool. No cotton — it wets out and won't dry.
- Polyester wicks fastest and is cheapest, but stinks. Merino resists odor and feels less clammy when wet, but costs more and wears holes faster at silk weight. Blends are the sweet spot in warm/humid climates.
- Fabric weight should be the lightest that stays comfortable at max exertion in the warmest conditions you'll see. Too heavy overheats; too light always needs a helper layer.
- Warm-weather cut: loose, wide neck, chest zip or button front. Cool-weather cut: semi-fitted, chest-zip vent, longer torso so the lower back isn't exposed bending over.
- Short sleeves for warm + low sun. Long sleeves for cooler temps, relentless sun, or scratchy brush.
- In bug country, a knit hiking shirt is inadequate — mosquitoes bite through and ticks walk in. The right answer is a permethrin-treated knit shirt, which keeps the airflow of a normal shirt. A tightly-woven "body armor" shirt works against mosquitoes but is stuffy and doesn't stop ticks.

**Picks**

- *Sierra Designs Long-Sleeve Pack Polo* — Skurka's Mountain-West go-to (sunny, dry, moderate sun).
- *Patagonia Daily Merino Polo* — merino blend for Appalachian heat and humidity.
- *Outdoor Research Echo Hoodie* — hooded lightweight poly; common template pick.
- **Bug shirt:** permethrin-treated knit from *ExOfficio BugsAway*, *Railriders Eco-Mesh*, *Columbia Insect Blocker*, or any shirt sent to *Insect Shield* for factory treatment (~$10). In peak bug season, a bug shirt *replaces* the standard hiking shirt rather than adding a layer.

## Pants or shorts

**Principles**

- Long pants are the versatile default. Their biggest job is protection (sun, brush, bugs) — not warmth. The added warmth of pants is often a liability, not a benefit. Switch to shorts only when it's genuinely hot and there are no bugs or sun concerns.
- Shorts, when worn, should be lined synthetic running shorts — the liner replaces underwear and moves moisture better than unlined hiking shorts. "Convertible" zip-off pants are heavier than plain pants and not worth the flexibility.
- Pants fabric: heavier stretch-woven nylon for cool temps and bushwhacking; lighter loosely-woven poly/nylon (vented if available) for sun/bug/mild-brush trips. Permethrin-treat light fabrics in peak bug season.
- Small spandex content adds stretch but retains water and slows drying. DWR on pants is marketing — gone after a few weeks of use.

**Picks**

- *Sierra Designs Stretch Cargo Pant* — cool weather, bushwhacking.
- *Railriders Eco-Mesh Pant* — full-length mesh vents, factory permethrin, hot and humid.
- *Prana Stretch Zion II* — common template pick, durable all-rounder.
- *Patagonia Strider Pro 5-inch* (or equivalent lined running short) — shorts default when shorts are appropriate.

## Underwear

**Principles**

- One pair. Boxer briefs in lightweight polyester or nylon with a little spandex. Hand-wash in water daily.
- Skip underwear entirely when wearing lined running shorts — the liner does the job.
- Tight compression shorts don't work as shorts (too warm under pants, too thin to stand alone).
- Fit is personal — try several.

**Picks**

- *ExOfficio Give-N-Go Sport Mesh 6-inch Boxer Brief* — Skurka's long-time default; stick with 6" inseam (3" rides up).
- For women, sources stay quiet on specifics but clients report success with *ExOfficio*, *Lululemon*, *Patagonia*, *Under Armour*.

## Sun hat / headwear

**Principles**

- A brimmed hat or ball cap is high-leverage: sun on scalp/ears, rain and sweat out of eyes, keeps shell and bivy mesh off face so bugs can't bite through, clipping point for a headlamp.
- In intense sun, choose a low-profile cap with an integrated neck/ear drape over a cowboy-style full-brim — the full brim fits poorly under a hood and catches wind.
- In low sun and deep forest, a visor with a sweatband is often enough (ballcap if you burn on top).
- A warm hat (wool or fleece beanie) belongs to the sleep system, not here — see `stop-and-sleep.md`. Ear coverage matters; chin-strap versions stay on inside a quilt.
- A mosquito head net is near-mandatory in bug country. Use mosquito mesh, not no-see-um mesh (no-see-um suffocates and blocks vision). Wear it over a ball cap so the netting doesn't touch skin.

**Picks**

- *Headsweats Supervisor* — sweatband visor, low-sun forests.
- *Headsweats Protech* — low-profile ball cap with neck drape, Skurka's high-sun default.
- *Headsweats Race Cap* — for thin-haired/burn-prone scalps under low sun.
- **Head net:** *Sea to Summit Mosquito Head Net* (longer neck seal). *Coghlan's* is cheap but too short.

## Sunglasses

**Principles**

- Not universal — East Coast forest and cloudy Alaskan routes can skip them. West, open terrain, or any snow warrants them.
- 100% UVA/UVB protection is the non-negotiable floor.
- Polarization helps with glare off water and snow. Photochromic lenses self-adjust to light levels — a real advantage over carrying interchangeable lenses, which are "a hassle in the field." Polarized + photochromic together is ideal but expensive, and polarization narrows the photochromic range.
- Optics/durability don't advance fast; last-season closeouts are real value (often one-third MSRP).
- Store in a hard case. For expensive pairs, the case is cheap insurance.

**Picks**

- *Julbo Dirt* with *Julbo Falcon* polarized photochromic lenses (9–20% VLT) — Skurka's long-term pair. Too dark for deep shade or heavy overcast; appropriate for open sun.
- Neutral-density gray lenses (any brand) are a reliable all-purpose choice when photochromic isn't available.

## Trekking poles

**Principles**

- Strongly recommended in mountainous terrain, with heavy packs, for river crossings, or any terrain with mud, sand, snow, or unstable footing. Measurably useful even on flat walking. Cut net leg load by up to ~25% by sharing work with arms.
- Bonus uses: pitch tarp/tent shelters (saving dedicated tent-pole weight), probe suspect snow bridges or silty puddles, fend off wildlife.
- Weight matters disproportionately: a pole swings thousands of times per day, so swing cost dwarfs carry cost. Aim under ~8 oz per pole or you'll start dragging them and walking out of rhythm.
- **Shaft:** carbon fiber if you can afford it — lighter, stiffer, warmer to touch. Aluminum bends under overload (still usable); carbon snaps (field-fix with splint + tape). Durability is more about how you treat them than material.
- **Lock:** lever lock over twist lock. Twist locks slip when cold or wet. Skip anti-shock mechanisms — heavier, cost more, squishy plant, rattle.
- **Grip:** cork (best) or high-density foam. Avoid rubber/plastic — heavy, slimy when wet, abrasive. Grip extensions below the main grip are highly recommended for quick choke-ups on steep pitches and sidehills.
- **Straps:** remove them. Lighter, unrestricted hand movement, and easier to release during an awkward plant so the pole doesn't break.
- **Tips and baskets:** carbide tips last ~1,500–2,000 miles. Remove the small 3-season baskets — they slow swing speed and snag brush. Snow baskets only for winter and early season.
- **Sizing:** elbow at 90° with forearm parallel to ground on level terrain. Lengthen for open flats, shorten or choke up on climbs and sidehills.
- **Technique:** opposite-leg rhythm on level ground (pole plants with the opposite foot). Uphill: swing each pole in concert with the opposite leg. Downhill: "palm" the top of the grip and swing each pole with the *same-side* leg so it contacts the ground a fraction before the leg and absorbs impact.

**Picks**

- *Black Diamond Alpine Carbon Cork* — Skurka's long-term favorite; the gold-standard lever-lock carbon pole. Lock has never failed him across thousands of miles.
- *Cascade Mountain Tech Quick Lock Carbon* — budget pick that holds up on rugged trips. Often at Costco.
