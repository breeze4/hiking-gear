# Packing — Backpack, Waterproofing, Food Storage, Stuff Sacks

The pack, its contents' organization, and food-storage strategy. Source: Skurka backpacks and food-storage chapters; Clelland Tips 62, 63, 108, 114; *Lighten Up!* backpack and food-storage chapters.

**Core discipline:** buy the pack **last**. Its size and load-carrying spec are driven by everything else you carry plus the maximum consumables load for the trip. Sizing the pack first is the most common mistake; it commits you to a weight and volume range before you know what you need.

**Discipline lever, not just a bag.** A small pack is a forcing function — it imposes discipline on every other gear decision. Both Clelland works say this directly: start with a small pack and your kit will shrink to fit.

---

## Pack

**Principles**

- **Target: 2–3 lb suspension pack for most 3-season backpackers.** This is Skurka's "sweet spot" — light enough to benefit from modern UL design, framed enough to carry food and a bear canister comfortably.
- **Framed vs. frameless cutoff: ~20–30 lb total pack weight.** Frameless wins below that on weight, cost, conformability, and range of motion. Above it, a frame transfers load to the hips (larger muscle groups, better center of gravity, more surface area) and the math tips toward framed.
- **Shape:** tall, narrow, shallow. Sits above the waist, doesn't hinder arm swing, keeps weight close to the spine. Distrust pear- or turtle-shell packs; the volume lives in the wrong place.
- **Frame types:** stays (aluminum/carbon rods), framesheets (plastic pane), or peripheral rods. For real load transfer the frame must be stiff, anchored into the hipbelt, and extend at least to the top of the shoulder straps.
- **Back panels:** basic pack fabric is lightest and sweatiest but carries best because the load is against your back. Mesh-covered foam is "false ventilation" — the mesh saturates. Trampoline/suspended mesh is the worst for center of gravity and fights a bear canister inside.
- **Closure:** top-loader, single main compartment. Lighter than panel-loading, easier to balance, no blowout-prone zippers. Top lids are fussy; roll-tops are clean and watertight if the fabric is.
- **Pockets:** dimensional side pockets beat flat stretch mesh on durability and volume. Side pockets should be reachable without taking the pack off. Hipbelt pockets earn their ounces.
- **Fabric:** 210D woven nylon (Cordura, Robic, Dyneema ripstop, X-Pac) is the practical sweet spot. 100D is fine for gentle use. Skurka is skeptical of DCF as a pack fabric — mediocre abrasion resistance for the price.
- **Hipbelt:** interchangeable belts sized to narrow waist ranges (e.g. 30–34") are the mark of a well-designed framed pack. Narrow belts make pressure points; too-wide belts cook and constrict.
- **Torso sizing:** when on the cusp between two sizes, downsize. With light loads the hipbelt floats up to the waist anyway.
- **Frameless virtual frame:** cinch the compression straps hard around a tightly-packed load; a closed-cell foam pad inside gives the pack cylindrical shape. "Removing pack compression straps is a classic stupid-light move" — Skurka.

**Picks**

- *ULA Circuit* (~2 lb 9 oz, ~$235) — template default; framed pack at the 2–3 lb sweet spot. Larger *Catalyst / Epic* for longer trips or bulky loads.
- *Sierra Designs Flex Capacitor* (~2.5 lb) — Skurka co-developed; 40–60 L adjustable gusset, accepts a bear canister, tough woven nylon.
- *Gossamer Gear, Six Moon Designs, ZPacks* framed packs — client-positive alternatives in the same tier.
- *Mountain Laurel Designs Exodus* (~16 oz, ~$195) — frameless standard for hikers staying under ~25 lb.
- *Ultimate Direction Fastpack* (~1 lb 9 oz) — FKT-style overnights and short trips.
- Skurka explicitly dismisses mainstream Osprey / Deuter / Gregory backpacking packs as "pounds heavier without matching performance." Fair warning for the template reader migrating from a traditional pack.

## Waterproofing

**Principles**

- **One 20-gallon trash compactor bag as a pack liner. No pack cover.** Unanimous between Skurka and Clelland.
- **Spec:** 2.2 oz, 2–2.5 mil polyethylene film, ~$1 each, lasts about a month of hard use before holes appear. White is easier than black for finding stuff deep in the pack.
- **Closure:** oversize the bag so there's excess at the top. Twist the excess into an "elephant's snout" and tuck it down into the side of the pack. No engineered seal; the twist works fine.
- **Layering the load against the liner:**
  - Inside the liner: everything that must stay dry — sleeping bag, spare clothes, puffy, sleeping clothes, electronics.
  - Outside the liner, *on top*: rain gear. The moment it rains you're going to put it on; don't bury it.
  - Outside the liner, *at the bottom*: a wet tarp in the morning. Drips drain into the pack body, not onto anything dry.
- **In very wet conditions:** two liners. One for gear you only need in camp (sleeping bag, inner tent), one for everything else.
- **Why not pack covers?** They don't cover the back-of-pack gap (water seeps in there), block pocket access, snag on brush, pool water in heavy rain, and are useless in river fords.
- **Dry sacks** (Granite Gear, Outdoor Research, Sea to Summit roll-tops) are legitimate for submersion-risk trips (boating) but heavier and costlier than a compactor bag for plain backpacking.

**Picks**

- *Hefty 18-gallon (or 20-gallon) trash compactor bag* — Clelland's spec. Cheap, light, effective.
- *Gossamer Gear Polycro* as an interior dry sack for items you want belt-and-suspenders on (electronics, down pieces). Minimal weight.

## Food storage: today

**Principles**

- Today's ration lives in a freezer-quart Ziploc at the top of the pack or in the top pocket. Easy access, minimal weight, disposable.
- Freezer-grade (not sandwich-grade) — they're tougher and the zipper survives multi-day use.

**Picks**

- *Ziploc Slider Freezer Quart* — the template default. Rotate with fresh bags every trip or two.

## Food storage: future days

**Principles**

- Opaque, odor-controlled bag buried deep in the pack. Protects the rest of the food from getting crushed or contaminated and keeps smells off the pack fabric (which matters both for bear response and for your own sanity).
- **Odor-barrier spec matters:** ordinary Ziploc doesn't block odor; you need a true odor-barrier bag. Effectiveness depends on keeping food crumbs off the outside of the bag.

**Picks**

- *LOKSAK OPSAK* (various sizes — 12×20" fits ~5 days of food at reasonable caloric density) — airtight, waterproof to 200 ft, the standard odor-barrier bag in all three books.

## Food protection (hard canister, Ursack, hang)

**Principles**

- **Decision tree by regulation and risk:**
  1. **Hard canister** where required by regulation or where bear concern is high. BearVault BV500 is the default; Bearikade carbon-fiber if budget allows.
  2. **Ursack AllWhite** where canisters aren't required but bears are a real concern. Spectra-fiber, IGBC-approved, ~8 oz, ~$70. Pair with an OPSAK inside for odor.
  3. **Mini-bear hang** (low branch, a few feet off the ground) where the threat is rodents and raccoons, not bears.
  4. **Sleep on it** where dispersed camping is allowed, bear concern is low, and the food bag doubles as a pillow or under-knees prop.
- **Bear hangs as bear protection are essentially obsolete.** Skurka's verdict: an effective hang is extremely hard, and textbook PCT/counter-balance/two-tree hangs are defeated by trained bears. Many land managers no longer accept hanging as a protection method. Hang only for mini-bears, and carry an Ursack if bears are in play.
- **Pack-shape implication:** hard canisters and curved-back packs fight each other. If you know you'll carry a canister, pick a pack with a flat back panel and a straight main compartment.
- **Canister side effects:** doubles as a camp stool; eats interior volume for the rest of the kit to work around.
- **Caloric density matters for food-storage sizing.** 120–140 cal/oz is realistic; 100 cal/oz means about 2 lb more food for a 7-day trip than a 140 cal/oz plan, enough to force a bigger pack or a second canister. Bagels-only doesn't fit; Snickers-only does.

**Picks**

- *BearVault BV500* — Skurka's canister default (~1 week of food). *BearVault BV450* is the CSV template reference for shorter trips.
- *Wild Ideas Bearikade Weekender / Expedition* — carbon-fiber; significantly lighter and pricier than BearVault.
- *Ursack AllWhite / Major* — the soft-sack pick where allowed.
- *Mini-bear hang kit:* 45–50 ft of 2 mm Spectra/Dyneema cord, a mesh throw bag, a tiny carabiner; under 3 oz total.
- *Bear spray*: *Counter Assault* in a hipbelt holster. Never buried in the pack, never clipped to the back.

## Stuff sacks

**Principles**

- **Avoid over-organizing.** Dedicated sacks for the things that need containment; gaps-in-the-pack filling for the things that don't.
  - *In sacks:* sleeping bag, cook pot, tent stakes, sunglasses (optional), and one general accessories sack (toiletries, lighter, first aid, light, fire starter, wallet).
  - *Not in sacks:* shelter, bug nest/bivy, and extra clothing. Use them to fill the empty spaces around the structured items, keeping the load conforming to the pack shape.
- Prefer **wide-opening** stuff sacks for small-item rummaging.
- **Multi-use:** a mosquito head net makes a great stuff sack for soft items like spare socks and a beanie (good visibility, not waterproof, doubles as a coffee filter — Clelland).
- **Zero-out anything pointless:** compression stuff sacks for sleeping bags (destroys loft, adds weight, unnecessary for a UL kit), hydration-bladder sleeves, sleeping-bag liners, dedicated potty trowels.

**Picks**

- *Hyperlite Mountain Gear Pods* — durable, roll-top, reasonable weight. Template default.
- *Sea to Summit Ultra-Sil Nano* — lightest standard stuff sack.
- *Granite Gear Air Bag* — heavier but nearly indestructible; good for the sleeping bag sack on long trips.

## Eyewear case

**Principles**

- Sources barely address this. Skurka's implicit method: keep glasses on the visor/hat when not worn; no case in the pack.
- If the trip involves river crossings, scrambling, or caving where the glasses have to go inside the pack, a hard case is cheap insurance — but don't pay a weight penalty on a typical 3-season trip.
- No principled "right answer" from source material; the CSV template note ("minimal weight to protect crucial & $$ item") is as far as it goes.

**Picks**

- Hard case included with sunglasses, *Julbo* included cases, and simple *padded slip cases* are all legitimate. Pick for the trip.

## Packing template

A single template works for most 3-season trips. The packing sequence shifts daily as food and water loads change, but the layering rule stays put.

1. **Heavy and dense** (food, water, fuel) against the back, at or below sternum level, centered along the spine. Cinch compression straps.
2. **Bottom of the pack** (inside the liner, unless wet): sleeping bag, future-day food.
3. **Outside the liner, bottom:** wet tarp, if packing in the rain.
4. **Middle of the pack:** clothing, shelter body, insulation — gap fillers.
5. **Top of the pack, inside the liner:** accessory sack, today's food in a freezer Ziploc, puffy, wind shirt.
6. **Top of the pack, outside the liner:** rain gear.
7. **Hipbelt pockets:** water purification, bug dope, head net, camera, lip balm, sunscreen.
8. **Side pockets:** water bottles, map, beanie, bear spray (if not on hipbelt), gloves.
9. **Shoulder straps:** overmitts clipped with a mini-carabiner; sunglasses on the hat when off the face.

Goal: walking within 15 minutes of waking, and no rummaging the main compartment during the day.
