# Element Protection — Storm Layer, Mid-Layer, Handwear, Headwear

Skurka's "Storm" tier plus the fleece mid-layer, liner gloves, shell mitts, and insulated headwear. This is the layer you add when weather turns, wind picks up, or the day cools off. Source: Skurka Ch. 4 (Items 7–9), Clelland Tips 49, 61, 107, 108, 110; *Lighten Up!* Clothing chapter.

**Core principle:** every piece in this category must layer over the Go Suit and under the Stop/Sleep layers. Size accordingly — shell over mid-layer over hiking shirt, liner gloves inside shell mitts. Nothing is evaluated alone.

**WP/B reality check:** all three sources agree that waterproof-breathable fabrics don't deliver on their label in sustained wet, especially when face-fabric DWR has degraded or when exertion is high. Ventilation (pit zips, core vents, front zip, sleeve roll) matters more than the membrane spec. Pick rain gear on trip humidity and storm duration, not on fabric marketing.

## Shell top (rain jacket)

**Principles**

- Bring one unless the forecast is bone dry and short. "Leaving rain gear home despite a forecast of 35 and rain" is Skurka's prototypical stupid-light move.
- Match the shell to the climate:
  - **Mountain West** (short storms, cool, low humidity): minimalist WP/B, ~6 oz, no features. It might not even get used, and a quick storm passes before the fabric fails.
  - **Humid East / PNW / Alaska** (sustained wet, warm, high exertion): prioritize ventilation over fabric spec. Assume you'll still get damp; compensate with a fleece mid-layer and dedicated sleeping clothes.
- Fit: layers over a hiking shirt + mid-layer, or over hiking pants + underwear. *Not* oversized to fit over a puffy — the puffy lives inside, not outside, for warmth and so it doesn't get wet.
- Avoid helmet-compatible hoods, pack-strap-blocked pockets, mesh-lined pockets, pit zips on a sub-6-oz shell (adds weight and failure points).

**Picks**

- *Outdoor Research Helium* — Skurka's feature-minimal Mountain West pick. ~6 oz, fits the "storm may pass before the fabric fails" rationale.
- *Montbell Versalite* — similar class, more ventilation and durability; common template pick for mixed 3-season.
- *The Packa* — Skurka's hybrid endorsement; jacket-poncho with sleeves and front zip that fits over the pack. Ventilated by design. 10 oz.
- *Frogg Toggs* / *DriDucks* — Clelland's ultralight fallback. Fragile (a few trips of life) but very cheap and very light. Good for short trips where weight matters and a rip doesn't ruin the kit.

## Shell bottoms (rain pants, rain skirt, rain chaps)

**Principles**

- Optional for most 3-season trips. Most UL hikers skip true rain pants; legs stay warm while moving and dry fast in nylon hiking pants.
- Carry them when the trip has sustained cold rain, wind chill at rest stops, or a mandatory standing-around context (guiding, fishing, photography).
- **Secondary job:** rain shell bottoms double as emergency leg insulation when stopped in wind. This is why a cheap set can be worth the weight even if it never actually rains.
- Rain kilt or rain chaps are a legitimate lighter alternative to full rain pants, especially paired with a poncho-style top.

**Picks**

- *Outdoor Research Helium Pants* — standard minimalist pairing with the Helium jacket.
- *ULA Rain Kilt* / *ZPacks Rain Kilt* — rain skirt; Clelland endorses a DIY version under 2 oz.
- *Rain chaps* — two silnylon leg tubes, lightest and least bulky leg rainwear; pair with a poncho that covers the gap chaps leave.

## Umbrella

**Principles**

- Not a gimmick. Legitimate 3-season tool in the right conditions: hot and humid with rain (stay cooler than a jacketed hiker), or open exposed desert (portable shade). Keeps the hood down so you can hear and don't overheat.
- Limits: catches wind, scant protection in driving rain, occupies an arm, snags on brush.
- Use cases to skip: anything with sustained high wind, off-trail through vegetation, or trips where you rely on both trekking poles continuously.

**Picks**

- *Gossamer Gear Lightflex* — Skurka's reference pick. ~8 oz, hand-held.
- *Six Moon Designs Silver Shadow* — common template pick. Reflective for desert shade use.
- *Montbell Trekking Umbrella* — lighter folding option.

## Mid-layer top (fleece)

**Principles**

- Skurka's position is firm: a polyester fleece mid-layer has two jobs, (a) standalone warmth when cool, (b) moisture-buffering insulation between a damp hiking shirt and a rain shell in sustained wet. A puffy can't do either job — puffies are less air-permeable, slower to dry, and lose loft when wet.
- Clelland and *Lighten Up!* functionally skip fleece for 3-season and roll its role into the puffy. Skurka's field lesson (Alaska Lost Coast gale, synthetic puffy "proved not to be warm when wet") is the reason to keep the dedicated fleece for anything with real wet-weather risk.
- 100-weight or 200-weight only. 300-weight is a jacket, not a mid-layer. Prefer polyester fleece over merino: cheaper, warmer per weight, absorbs less water, dries faster.
- Avoid spandex content, wind-blocking membranes (Windstopper), abrasion panels, full zippers, and excessive pockets. All add weight without warmth.
- Fleece is not interchangeable with a windshirt. A windshirt has its own narrow role (dry-weather cool-breeze insulation) but doesn't buffer moisture or provide warmth when wet.

**Picks**

- *Patagonia R1 Pullover* — Skurka's benchmark. ~12 oz, grid fleece, half-zip. Works as mid-layer and standalone.
- *Marmot Rocklin 1/2 Zip* — ~10 oz budget pick with the same role.
- *Patagonia Houdini* — windshirt Skurka considered but rejected for the fleece slot. Pack it as a separate item for dry trips, not as a fleece substitute.

## Trekking pants or thermals

**Principles**

- "Hiking pants" in this slot means a second leg layer beyond the Go Suit — usually for cool-temp rest stops, cold mornings, bug/brush protection, or sun coverage when going from shorts to pants.
- *Lighten Up!* treats a thermal long-john bottom under hiking pants as the default 3-season cold-leg answer: insulation while moving, plus rain pants over top for standing-around warmth in wind.
- If a second pair of pants is carried, size it to layer *over* the first pair.
- DWR on pants is marketing — gone after a few weeks of use. Don't pay for it.

**Picks**

- *Sierra Designs Stretch Cargo Pant* — heavier stretch-woven nylon for cool temps and bushwhacking.
- Any *polyester / merino long john bottom* as the thermal layer — no need for a fancy brand, the job is dry warmth under another layer.

## Liner gloves

**Principles**

- The universal first tier of handwear. Warmth, dexterity, and abrasion resistance — especially against pole grips, which eat pure-fleece or pure-wool gloves quickly.
- Standalone cutoff for most hikers: running at ~30°F, hiking at ~40°F. Below that, add a shell mitt.
- Clelland's point: liners insulate even when wet, which is what you want for hiking through sustained rain.
- Avoid tight wrists and tight cuffs — cold hands follow impaired circulation.

**Picks**

- *DeFeet DuraGlove ET Wool* — Skurka's long-term pick. ~3 oz, 52% nylon content plus silicone palm/finger logos drive the durability. 4–6 weeks of daily backpacking; pure-fleece/wool/poly gloves last about half as long.
- *Smartwool liner* or *OR PL 100* — serviceable alternatives.

## Shell mitts

**Principles**

- Added over liner gloves in cold precip or chilling wind. The mitt's job is wind and water resistance, not insulation — the liner handles warmth.
- **Avoid WP/B shell mitts for pole users.** Pole-grip abrasion destroys waterproofness fast, and the membrane traps sweat anyway. A simple waterproof/nonbreathable shell (even a gardening glove in the right material) is fully vented by pulling it off — "I've never complained about having hot hands when it's 40 and raining."
- Mitts are warmer than gloves for the same weight; you don't need fingers inside a shell.
- Emergency improvisation: extra socks as thumbless mittens, a stuff sack as an overmitt. Do it rather than let fingers get cold.

**Picks**

- *Showa TEMRES 281 / 282 / 282-02* — waterproof garden/fishing gloves, absurdly cheap and functional. The commonly-cited cold-and-wet shell mitt pick.
- *Luke's Ultralight / Outdoor Research / ZPacks* taped-seam shell mitts — $60–80, 1–3 oz/pair. Lighter but less abrasion-tolerant.

## Insulated headwear

**Principles**

- The warm hat is load-bearing in both the rest-stop warmth budget and the sleep system. *Lighten Up!* is most explicit: it belongs with the sleeping bag decision, not floating alone.
- Best warm hats cover the ears. For cold sleepers and quilt users, a chin strap (or a beanie sized tight enough to stay put) keeps the hat on through the night.
- A sun hat (see `worn.md`) is a separate item, not a substitute — it doesn't insulate.
- Progression: Buff → fleece or wool beanie → lightweight balaclava as temps drop. Full Arctic stack (balaclava + shell hood + parka hood) is out of 3-season scope.

**Picks**

- *Buff Original* — multi-use polyester tube; headband, beanie, neck gaiter, dust filter. ~2 oz. Skurka's first insulating layer.
- *Smartwool merino beanie* or similar — dedicated warm hat for colder nights.
- *Outdoor Research / Black Diamond lightweight balaclava* — escalate here when temps go below freezing.

## What sources stay quiet on
