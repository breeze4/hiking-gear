# Sleep — Bag or Quilt, Pad, Pillow, Ear Plugs

The nighttime warmth and rest system. Source: Skurka "Sleeping bags & pads" chapter; Clelland Tips 48, 93, 96–100; *Lighten Up!* sleeping systems chapter.

**Core principle: sleep is a system, not a bag.** Nighttime warmth is the product of bag/quilt + pad + shelter + clothing + site selection + metabolism (food, hydration, fatigue, acclimatization). The temperature rating on the bag is one input, not the answer. Wear your insulated clothing inside the bag — it means a lighter bag does the job. See `stop-and-sleep.md` for the clothing side of this system.

**Learn your bag on low-stakes trips first.** Skurka's explicit how-to: backyard or quick overnight → range of temperatures → combinations with different pads, clothing, shelters. A sleeping bag you haven't tested is a liability.

## Bag or quilt

**Principles**

- **Quilt is the 3-season default for the weight-conscious hiker.** All three sources agree. Rationale: a mummy bag wastes ~30% of its potential warmth because the insulation under the sleeper is crushed flat and can't loft. A quilt drops the useless bottom and adds variable girth so it adapts to different clothing and temperature levels.
- **Quilt weaknesses and their fixes:** drafts and no hood.
  - Wear hooded clothing at night (wind shirt hood, puffy hood, warm hat with chin strap).
  - Use a full-sided shelter, or complement an open tarp with a water-resistant bivy sack.
  - Be a calm sleeper (Skurka literally trained himself to stop twisting).
  - Buy the quilt wide enough — extra girth adds a few ounces but slays draftiness.
- **Mummy bag is the fallback** when the quilt doesn't work: consistently below ~30°F, restless sleepers, high-wind campsites, or trips where the margin for error is tight. The hood and full enclosure are a real advantage in cold. Accept the 30% underside waste as the price for the security.
- **Top bags** (mummy-style with a pad-attached bottom) are a niche — they solve pad-roll for some sleepers but feel like being "strapped to a gurney" to others. Sierra Designs Backcountry Beds are the most interesting example, with an integrated comforter that addresses mummy claustrophobia.
- **Down is the 3-season insulation of choice.** Better warmth/weight, much better compressibility, and multi-year lifespan vs. synthetic's single-long-trip life. Synthetic is cheaper and slightly more forgiving in sustained wet. Water-resistant down (DownTek, DriDown, Down Defender, Encapsil) has largely closed the wet-weather gap. Skurka's summary: a moisture-degraded high-end down performs about as well as a perfectly dry high-end synthetic.
- **Don't let it get soaked, but don't panic about mild dampness either.** Protection discipline: waterproof pack liner, periodic sun-drying every few days, ventilated shelter, separate waterproof sack inside the pack on wet trips.
- **Shell fabric matters more than people think.** Below ~1.0 oz/sq yd is the premium sweet spot — a bag can drop 15% of its weight by switching shell fabric. The fabric must be slightly air-permeable so moisture can escape and the insulation doesn't condensate from inside. WP/B shell fabrics are counterproductive for the same reason.
- **Temperature ratings are approximate.** EN 13537 is better than manufacturer self-ratings but is still a ballpark. Interpret Comfort as "a cold sleeper is OK," Limit as "a warm sleeper is OK." Ignore the Extreme rating entirely — don't buy a bag based on the temperature at which you'd become hypothermic but survive.
- **Loft rule of thumb from *Lighten Up!*:** 2.5" of quilt loft is comfortable for an "average" sleeper to ~24°F. A warm sleeper takes the same quilt to ~15°F; a cold sleeper starts chilling at ~35°F. Quilts only have loft on top, so 2.5" of quilt loft ≈ 5" of sleeping bag loft. Women typically sleep colder than men.
- **Core 13 sizing target:** aim for a quilt or bag comfortable to ~20°F for typical 3-season shoulder conditions; push lighter (40°F quilt) only on trips where nighttime lows are reliably above freezing and the layering system can absorb a cold snap.

## Pad or under-quilt

**Principles**

- **The pad's primary job is blocking conductive heat loss to the ground, not cushioning.** R-value is the only measure of this, and it's linear: R-4 is four times as warm as R-1. Pad warmth is additive when you stack two pads.
- **R-value target for 3-season:** ~3.0+ for most trips. Below that, shoulder-season cold snaps get miserable. Above ~4.5, you're in winter territory and paying weight for capacity you don't need.
- **Cushioning is only necessary on hard-packed ground.** On pine needles, grass, moss, or duff, a thin pad can be more comfortable than a plush pad on compacted high-use dirt — and the organic material is less thermally conductive so you stay warmer too. The "pick your sleeping spot" discipline buys you pad weight savings.
- **Pad types:**
  - **Closed-cell foam** — cheapest, most reliable (no leaks, no punctures), trimmable, modifiable, doubles as a virtual pack frame in a frameless pack, doubles as a sit pad, doubles as an emergency splint. Bulky, limited rebound (flattens in 6–8 weeks of nightly use), lowest R-value per weight.
  - **Air pads** (insulated) — warmest for weight when new, most compact, most comfortable. Expensive, prone to leaks and delamination, must be pump-inflated (not breath-inflated) to avoid moisture degrading the internal insulation. In cold, you need to top them off overnight as the air cools and contracts.
  - **Self-inflating** — supplanted by air pads on every metric except cost. Skip.
- **Torso-length pad + pack under the legs** is a legitimate UL move. Clelland and Skurka both do this. Extend with a folded closed-cell strip under the knees, or insulate the legs with the pack, maps, and food bag.
- **Sleep pad discipline:** inspect the sleeping spot for sharp thorns and pointy rocks before laying down the pad. Carry a patch kit — every air pad punctures eventually, and field repair is easy.
- **Under-quilt (hammock only):** suspended beneath a hammock to insulate the underside, since a pad inside a hammock squirrels around and compresses. No value outside a hammock system. See `shelter.md` for hammock systems more generally.

## Pillow

**Principles**

- **All three sources say: improvise.** The default UL pillow is a stuff sack filled with spare clothing (typically a puffy, wind shirt, or rain jacket) and placed in a natural incline in the ground. Commercial camping pillows are fine but rarely weight-competitive.
- **Support under the knees** is as important as under the head for back-sleepers — prevents lower-back stress and knee hyperextension. A food bag, stuff sack, or rolled puffy can serve.
- **Soft-sided water bottles** (Platypus, CNOC) filled three-quarters with air work as a pillow inside a stuff sack with clothing.
- **Clelland's Ziploc-baggie pillow:** 7 partially-inflated freezer Ziplocs in a small stuff sack. ~1.8 oz total. Works. Requires stout double-zipper bags — sandwich-grade ones deflate under head weight.
- **The puffy-as-pillow trick** serves double duty: pillow during sleep, instantly accessible if you wake up cold. *Lighten Up!* specifically calls this out.
- **Site-dependent improvisations:** pinecones in the pack, sand in the pack, tiny pebbles. Only if the raw material is right at camp.
- **Commercial air pillows** (Sea to Summit Aeros, Klymit X, Nemo Fillo) are legitimate for hikers who can't sleep on improvised pillows. Budget ~2–3 oz.

**Picks**

- *Spare clothing in a stuff sack* — zero weight, always works.
- *Sea to Summit Aeros Ultralight* — ~2 oz, small commercial pick.
- *Klymit X Pillow* — common template default for users who want a real pillow.
- *Nemo Fillo Elite* — fabric-topped for face comfort; heavier at ~3 oz.

## Ear plugs

**Principles**

- **Sources silent.** None of Skurka, Clelland, or *Lighten Up!* discuss ear plugs. There is no principled guideline from source material.
- Practical guidance (not from sources): foam plugs are negligible weight and real utility for group trips, Arctic summer daylight, windy alpine camps, and campsites near loud water. Bring them if noise wakes you up; skip them if it doesn't.

**Picks**

- *Mack's Pillow Soft Foam* — common template pick; soft foam, multi-use.
- *Hearos Xtreme Protection* — higher NRR for noise-sensitive sleepers.

## Site selection is part of the sleep system

Three books, one rule: pick the sleeping spot like it matters, because it does.

- **Soft organic ground** (pine needles, moss, grass, duff, leaves) is warmer (lower thermal conductivity than compacted dirt or rock) *and* more comfortable than any pad.
- **Avoid cold-air sinks.** Meadow floors, valley bottoms, and drainage lines collect cold air on calm nights — dew forms there too. Move up-slope a few feet for a real temperature bump.
- **Dense foliage holds heat** on clear, cold nights. A packed zone of trees or dense brush is warmer than an open clearing, and the coldest nights are the clearest.
- **Flat-test by lying down** in the dark. Clelland: "you'll know right away if it's lumpy or tilted." Foolproof method, advocated emphatically.
- **Landscape small** before pitching. A few minutes sweeping out pinecones and flattening duff costs nothing and buys real sleep quality.

## Wear-it-all-to-bed protocol

Clelland's Tip 99 and Skurka's "clothing is part of the sleep system" thesis converge here:

1. Climb into the bag or quilt **warm**, not cold. Jumping jacks beforehand if needed.
2. Wear every insulation layer you carried — bandana, puffy, wind shirt, warm hat, gloves, liner socks over sleeping socks. No "saving" layers.
3. Eat a big dinner. Metabolism requires calories; a well-fed sleeper is a warm sleeper.
4. Close the hood, cinch the quilt, position the warm hat *before* getting in. Don't wait until 2am to discover the closure system.
5. **Do not wear wet gear to bed.** If rain gear is soaked, leave it in a soggy pile under the shelter. The ambient air during rain is usually warmer than a cold clear night, so you don't need it. Wet down in the bag kills the bag.
6. Mid-night emergency warmth: crunches or sit-ups inside the quilt will visibly raise core temperature in a few minutes. If you're doing this every night, you've undercut the sleep system — correct it on the next trip.
