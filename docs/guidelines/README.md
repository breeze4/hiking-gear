# Gear Guidelines — Cross-Cutting Principles

These are the shared ideas that govern every category file in this directory. Read this once; then each `<category>.md` file assumes you already understand the framework and extends it with category-specific decisions.

**Sources.** Three books, cited throughout:

- **Skurka** — Andrew Skurka, *The Ultimate Hiker's Gear Guide* (2nd ed., National Geographic, 2017).
- **Clelland Tips** — Mike Clelland, *Ultralight Backpackin' Tips* (2011).
- **Lighten Up!** — Don Ladigin (illustrated by Mike Clelland), *Lighten Up!* (2014).

**Scope.** 3-season backpacking. Winter, desert, and alpine edge cases are called out at the bottom of each file and deferred to override subdirectories (`desert/`, `alpine/`) which contain only the files that genuinely differ from baseline.

---

## Pack-weight bands and targets

All three books define base weight the same way: everything in the pack minus consumables (food, water, fuel). Clelland's bands (Tip 11):

- **Traditional** — base weight over 20 lb; pack weight usually over 35 lb.
- **Lightweight** — base weight below 20 lb. "Totally realistic with minimal dedication."
- **Ultralight (UL)** — base weight under 10 lb.
- **Sub-ultralight (SUL)** — base weight below 5 lb. "The domain of passionate zealots."

Ladigin draws the same bands in *Lighten Up!*. Skurka declines to codify bands ("I will not present arbitrary pack weight guidelines") but gives a concrete hiker-style target: ~10 lb base weight "for normal three-season conditions."

All three converge on **~10 lb base weight as the 3-season dedicated-hiker target**, with the 10–20 lb band treated as an accessible entry point.

## Hiking vs. camping as the primary frame

Skurka's strongest framing device: "there are styles of backpacking, differentiated by the time and focus placed on hiking versus camping. Trips can be entirely hiking- or camping-centric, or some balance of the two."

Backcountry **hikers** optimize for forward progress with minimal kit and rely on skill. Backcountry **campers** optimize for comfort at a base camp, carry more, and can afford to pack their fears. Clelland frames the same split: "find comfort on the trail vs. find comfort in camp only after jettisoning the pack."

**These guidelines assume a hiking-leaning user** unless a specific file calls out a camping-centric override. If you're camping-first, treat weight targets here as ceilings, not goals, and prioritize in-camp comfort where the books explicitly permit it.

## Systems thinking: everything overlaps

Both Clelland and Skurka explicitly reject gear-as-independent-items.

> No piece of gear should be used independently; everything is part of a system. I don't have cook gear; I have a cook system. Same with the shelter and packing, and they should all overlap because of multiuse items. — Clelland, Tip 21

The practical consequences:

- **Never evaluate a sleeping bag in isolation from the shelter and the clothing.** A quilt + bivy + puffy combination can be equivalent to a warmer mummy bag alone. The warm hat belongs with the sleeping-bag decision. The rain jacket belongs with the windshirt decision.
- **Clothing is a four-tier layering framework** (Skurka):
  - **Go** — what you hike in. See `worn.md`.
  - **Storm** — what you add when weather turns. See `element-protection.md`.
  - **Stop** — what you add when you stop moving. See `stop-and-sleep.md`.
  - **Sleep** — dedicated dry clothing kept in the pack for nights in wet climates. Also `stop-and-sleep.md`.
- **Everything in the clothing system should be serviceable together.** Shell sized over mid-layer, mid-layer over hiking shirt, liner gloves inside shell mitts. Fit constraints flow from the system, not from each piece in isolation.
- **Sleep is a system, not a bag.** Nighttime warmth = bag/quilt + pad + shelter + clothing + site selection + metabolism. See `sleep.md`.

## Clelland's core manifesto (Tips 1–10)

The compact credo that underwrites every specific decision. Paraphrased:

1. **Get a scale.** Non-negotiable first step. A $20–35 digital postal scale with 0.1 oz resolution.
2. **Comfortable and safe are vital.** "Anyone can go out into the mountains with a tiny amount of gear and suffer." The goal is a light kit *and* being warm, dry, fed, and safe.
3. **Scrutinize everything.** Every item gets weighed, held, questioned. Will I be fine without this? Is there a lighter option? Is it multi-use? Can something else do the same job?
4. **Make your own stuff; from trash is best.** Cat-food-can stoves, PET soda bottles, homemade rain skirts. UL does not have to be expensive.
5. **It's okay to be nerdy.** Obsessing over half an ounce is fine and encouraged.
6. **Try something new every trip.** Never settle; keep iterating.
7. **Simply take less stuff.** The fastest way to get an item's weight to zero is to leave it home. The honest answer must be YES or NO, never *maybe*.
8. **Know the difference between wants and needs.** "You actually NEED very little."
9. **Cut stuff off your gear.** Strap tails, soda-bottle rings, tent-stake excess. The weight saved is trivial; the mindset shift is not.
10. **Document your gear.** Weigh everything, write it on each item with a Sharpie, maintain a spreadsheet with a "why" column. If you can't answer *why*, don't take it.

Tips 2 and 7 together — the "comfort/safety floor" and the "zero-weight item" — underwrite most specific decisions in these guidelines.

## Stupid light vs. genuinely light

Skurka's term for weight-saving that crosses the safety or comfort floor. His definition:

> The counterproductive sacrifice of comfort, safety, and fun to save weight. For example, leaving behind rain gear despite a forecast of 35 degrees and rain, or using toothpick-size tent stakes that easily pull out.

Concrete stupid-light calls Skurka makes in the book:

- **Leaving sleeping clothes home on a wet-climate trip.** "Sure, it can be a one-pound weight-savings, but what is the cost — to your body and your morale — of a crappy night of sleep?"
- **Under-sized cook pot.** "The weight difference between a 16-ounce and 32-ounce pot is minor, but the convenience factor is huge."
- **Removing pack compression straps.** "Removing compression straps is a classic 'stupid light' move."

The rule: **when the weight savings is small and the failure mode is hard to recover from in the field, accept the ounces.**

## Shared principles across all three books

- **Weigh everything, or you're guessing.** Clelland Tip 1, *Lighten Up!* Principle 2.
- **Multi-use beats single-purpose.** All three books, repeatedly. A bandana replaces a pot grip, a washcloth, and a coffee filter. A trekking pole is also a shelter pole and a food-hang anchor.
- **Heaviest items first.** Clelland Tip 15 "Big Three/Four" (pack, shelter, sleep, cook) and *Lighten Up!* Principle 4 ("Look at the heaviest items first"). Don't saw the toothbrush in half before you've addressed the pack.
- **No redundant items.** One hiking shirt, one puffy, one pair of underwear. Redundancy lives in dedicated sleeping clothes (when wet-climate requires it), not in duplicated Go Suit items.
- **Pack for the conditions, not your fears.** Skurka's "know before you go" research discipline; Clelland Tip 20 "There's no such thing as 'just in case.'" Replace "just in case" with specific condition-driven decisions.
- **Knowledge weighs nothing.** Comfort and safety depend on "the weightless backcountry knowledge I could carry between my ears" (Skurka), not on redundant gear. *Lighten Up!*: the lightweight hiker is "highly self-reliant."
- **Rain gear fails wet.** All three are skeptical of WP/B ratings after moderate use; Skurka is the most explicit ("wets out after moderate use due to failure of the DWR coating"). See `element-protection.md` for the full treatment.
- **Shared gear for teams.** Cook gear, shelter, repair kit, first-aid, water treatment, maps, and compass are all divisible by the number of people. Plan by group, not by individual.

## What the guidelines don't cover

- **Winter backpacking.** Sub-freezing nights, snow travel, VBL systems, mukluks, neoprene overboots, four-season mountaineering shelters. Out of scope — read the source chapters directly.
- **Packrafting, canoeing, or water-travel trips.** Out of scope.
- **Technical climbing, mountaineering, or glacier travel.** Ice axes, crampons, ropes, harnesses — covered only as override hints in `tools-first-aid.md`.
- **Canyoneering.** Activity-specific; if the user actually needs it, write `canyoneering.md` separately.
- **Ultra-aggressive SUL approaches** (sub-5-lb base weight). The books describe them but none endorse them as the default; these guidelines target the ~10 lb Skurka-hiker zone.
- **Bug-season overrides** — permethrin-treated pants, bug-shirt leg layers, head nets as primary-use items. Belongs in a bug-season override file.
- **Food, nutrition, and caloric density.** Touched on in `pack.md` (food storage); full treatment is out of scope.

Override subdirectories (`desert/`, `alpine/`) contain only the files that genuinely differ from baseline. A missing override file means "baseline applies as-is."

## How to use this directory

1. **Start here** — the cross-cutting principles govern every specific decision.
2. **Pick the category file** for the item you're making a decision about.
3. **Read the Principles section first** to understand the rules. Read the Picks section only after you've decided what category of answer you want.
4. **Check the override subdirectories** for your trip type. If `desert/shelter.md` exists, read it as a delta on `shelter.md`. If it doesn't exist, baseline shelter advice applies.
5. **Look for the "What sources stay quiet on" or "Deferred to override" sections** — these tell you where the guidelines stop and your own judgment or external research needs to start.

The Picks lists are exemplars, not prescriptions. They show what the source books endorse as concrete instances of the principles. Substitute equivalents freely — the principles are load-bearing, the brand names are not.
