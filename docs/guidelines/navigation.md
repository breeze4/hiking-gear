# Navigation — Maps, Compass, Watch, GPS

Wayfinding resources and tools. Source: Skurka "Navigation" chapter (the most substantive treatment in any source); Clelland Tips 34, 57, 61, 75; *Lighten Up!* "Small Necessities."

**Core principle: skill beats gear.** All three sources say this explicitly. The best navigation kit in the world doesn't "stay found" for you — that's the work of consulting the map constantly, matching terrain to contours, and paying attention. Carry the right tools, then practice with them.

**Core 3-season navigation system** (Skurka's template):

1. **Small-scale topographic map** — the primary, always-accessible resource.
2. **Large-scale overview map** — for macro route decisions.
3. **Datasheet / databook** — optional, for prescribed itineraries.
4. **Route description / guidebook** — optional, for context and tricky sections.
5. **Watch with altimeter** — for dead reckoning and ruling out false summits.
6. **Magnetic baseplate compass** — for bearings and orientation.
7. **GPS (smartphone with app)** — as an ace in the sleeve, not the primary.

You don't need every component on every trip. Strip the system to the minimum that the trip demands — but strip *down* deliberately, not from laziness.

## Guidebook / route description

**Principles**

- Optional. A good small-scale topo with a few deliberate annotations is "worth a thousand words" — you don't need prose to tell you the distance to a shelter or which way to turn.
- A guidebook earns its weight for: general context about a section, historical or ecological background, tricky passages that need text to explain, permit logistics, and resupply info for long trips.
- **For routes with no published guidebook, make your own.** Consolidate information from email threads, forums, trip reports, land-manager websites, and your own reservations into a single printed document. This is Skurka's standard practice on off-trail routes he later publishes.
- **Guidebooks are good sharing candidates** — one per group is enough. Don't hand the only copy to someone who might get separated.

**Picks**

- *Trail-specific published guidebooks* (e.g., Andrew Skurka's *Kings Canyon High Basin Route*, Alan Dillman's *Yosemite High Route Guide*, PCT/CDT/AT handbooks) — trip-specific.
- *Homemade PDFs printed at FedEx Office* — Skurka's practice for custom routes. Full color, double-sided, 24-lb paper.

## Topographic maps

**Principles**

- **The single most useful navigation resource, always.** Keep maps accessible — side pocket or hipbelt — not buried in the pack. Consult them constantly to "stay found." Rule: if you haven't looked at the map in an hour, look at it.
- **Match scale to terrain and on-trail/off-trail choice.**
  - **1:24,000 (USGS 7.5-minute quads)** — the gold standard for off-trail or very low-use trails in the lower 48. 40-ft contour intervals; one inch = 0.38 mile. "Excessively detailed" for well-established trails but unmatched for off-trail precision.
  - **1:100,000** — Clelland's default for most on-trail use. Lighter, fewer sheets, enough detail for maintained trails.
  - **1:250,000** — Alaska and other areas where finer scales aren't available.
- **Two sets of maps for serious trips:** small-scale (1:24,000) for precise navigation of the current section, plus large-scale (1:100,000 or private recreation maps) for macro route decisions like "where am I camping tomorrow" or "how do I reroute around that snowfield."
- **Private recreation maps** (Beartooth Publishing, DeLorme, McKenzie Maps, National Geographic, Tom Harrison) often beat USGS large-scale overviews — more current trail data, designated campsites, service locations, land-manager boundaries.
- **CalTopo is the workflow tool.** Annotate the route, measure distance and elevation, export a multi-page 11×17" PDF, print at a local FedEx for full-color double-sided 24-lb paper. 11×17 shows ~15× more at a given zoom than a 5-inch phone screen.
- **Landsat imagery** reveals vegetation density, use trails, and recent topographical changes (landslides, shifted river channels). Rarely worth printing; annotate key details onto the topo and carry the imagery on the phone for reference.
- **Skill-building:** get to "intermediate map-reader" before attempting hard off-trail. That means orient the map, match contours to terrain, pinpoint your location from features and dead reckoning, and — critically — stop "bending the map" to match where you think you are.
- **Trim maps** to reduce weight, but leave enough margin for emergency reroutes. Clelland learned this the hard way.
- **Max weight (Clelland's template):** ~4 oz for the full map set on a typical trip.

**Picks**

- *USGS 7.5-minute quads* (printed from CalTopo or USGS Store) — off-trail default.
- *National Geographic Trails Illustrated* — printed recreation maps for national parks, forests, monuments. 1:40,000 to 1:100,000 depending on area.
- *Beartooth Publishing / Tom Harrison / McKenzie / DeLorme* recreation maps — regional specialty publishers, often the best overview map for a specific area.
- *Trail-specific map sets* (Pfiffner Traverse Guide, Skurka's route guides) — custom scales calibrated to the route.

## Databook / datasheet

**Principles**

- A prescribed-route tool. A datasheet lists key landmarks (intersections, passes, creek crossings, shelters) with incremental and cumulative distances; optionally elevation, lat/lon, vertical change, and average travel time.
- **No value for a spontaneous itinerary.** High value for thru-hikes and specific custom routes. Used for:
  - Dead reckoning to the next landmark.
  - Identifying realistic camping areas for tonight and future nights.
  - Tracking pace — am I ahead or behind? Should I add a side trip or ration calories?
- **Reconfigurable as a specialized list:**
  - **Water chart** (desert): water source locations, reliability, quality, distances to the next source. The most important datasheet variant for arid travel.
  - **Resupply list** (thru-hike): mileage markers, mail-drop instructions, estimated arrival dates, town services.
- Most datasheet info is derived from the topo map and could in theory be recalculated in the field. In practice, digital measurement (CalTopo) is more accurate, and a printed sheet holds far more points than you could memorize.

**Picks**

- *Route guide datasheets* (Skurka's route guides include them; published thru-hike guides include them).
- *Custom CalTopo datasheets* — build your own with CalTopo's measure tools and export.

## Watch (with altimeter)

**Principles**

- **A simple timepiece alone earns its place** for break-planning, group communication, and dead reckoning (pace and arrival-time estimates).
- **Altimeter watches earn more weight in mountainous terrain.** Knowing elevation lets you:
  - Rule out false summits and passes.
  - Confirm uncertain landmarks (unmarked trail junction at 10,600 ft — is this it?).
  - Contour a slope at a specific elevation instead of wandering up and down looking for the right bench.
- **GPS sport watches** consolidate timepiece + altimeter + barometer + digital compass + track recorder in one unit. Useful if you already own one for training; not worth buying just for navigation.
- **Recalibrate the altimeter at known elevations** (a signed pass, a marked trail junction, a lake at a known altitude). Altimeter drift over a day in changing pressure can be 100+ feet.
- **The watch counts as worn weight**, not base weight — relevant to how you record it on the gear list.

**Picks**

- *Suunto Core* — Skurka's baseline altimeter pick; ~3 oz, barometer, digital compass, storm alarm.
- *Suunto Ambit Peak / Ambit3 Peak* — Skurka's own GPS sport watch; 3 oz, customizable screens, track recording. Doubles as a training tool.
- *Garmin Fenix* series — GPS sport watch alternative; overkill for casual use, great for ultramarathoners.
- *Any basic digital watch* — if you don't need altimeter data, the timepiece alone is fine.

## Compass

**Principles**

- **Baseplate, liquid-filled, adjustable declination.** That's the spec. All three sources agree.
- Additional features worth having: fast needle, 1- or 2-degree graduations, luminous markers for night use, global needle (tilt tolerance) for international or tilted use.
- **Sighting mirror is unnecessary for navigation.** Skurka flatly rejects it; *Lighten Up!* notes the mirror earns weight only as a dual-use item (contact lens insertion, tick checks, signaling).
- **Five operations a compass actually does:** orient the map; take a field bearing; take a map bearing; transpose a field bearing to the map; transpose a map bearing to the field. Triangulation (three bearings for a position fix) is rare in practice — Skurka says "pay better attention and simply stay found" instead.
- **A compass is safety gear.** Carry it even when you don't expect to need it. *Lighten Up!* explicitly classifies it among safety items that return to the "always packed" pile regardless of whether the last trip used them. Skurka's stories: whiteout pass descent in the Absarokas, Kenai bushwhack, Utah canyoneering.
- **Read the instructions carefully, maybe twice.** A compass you haven't practiced with is not a navigation tool, it's a weight.

**Picks**

- *Suunto M-3G Global Pro* — Skurka's favorite. $80, 2 oz, 20° global needle tolerance.
- *Suunto M-3D* — budget sibling of the M-3G; requires perfectly level holding.
- *Silva Ranger 515* or *Brunton TruArc 3* — common template alternatives at the same spec.
- *Keychain button compass* — worth it only if you need "rough north" and already have a better compass in the kit. Not a primary.

## Handheld GPS or GPS app

**Principles**

- **Smartphone + app is the right answer for most people.** No extra weight (you already carry the phone), superior screen and layers, huge storage for map/imagery data. Handheld GPS units (Garmin, et al.) are worse in every dimension *except* weather and battery life.
- **GPS is a supplement, not a replacement for map reading.** Skurka is emphatic. The GPS tells you straight-line distance and bearing to a landmark; it does not tell you the line of least resistance — through brush, canyons, or river fords.
- **Use cases where a GPS earns its weight:**
  - Confirming position when paper maps hit their resolution limit.
  - Storing imagery for areas outside your printed map set (accidental route deviations).
  - Quickly resolving junctions or campsites in dense forest with no distant landmarks.
  - Recording a track for later review or sharing.
- **Battery management:**
  - Airplane mode + cell radio off + occasional use → a typical smartphone lasts a week.
  - Continuous use → bring a portable charger (Anker PowerCore or equivalent).
  - Cold kills battery — keep the phone in an inside pocket in sub-freezing conditions.
- **Ruggedness:** a smartphone is less weather-/impact-resistant than a dedicated handheld. A simple case plus a ziplock sleeve handles most 3-season weather.
- **Glove operability:** touchscreens are miserable in gloves. Note this as a constraint for cold-weather use; swap to a tactile handheld if cold hands will be a primary use case.

**Picks**

- *Gaia GPS* — Skurka's app pick. Layers include USGS, Landsat, MVUM, private recreation maps. Subscription-based.
- *CalTopo mobile* — native CalTopo app; same data as the web tool, offline download.
- *OnX Backcountry* — common template alternative; strong map layers for hunting and recreation.
- *Garmin GPSMAP 66i / inReach Explorer+* — handheld GPS with satellite communicator built in. Overlap with the sat-comm slot in `tools-first-aid.md`. The better answer if you need both and don't want to carry two devices.

## Paper protection

**Principles**

- **Gallon freezer Ziplocs are the default.** Skurka's setup: two bags. One holds today's materials and lives in a pack side pocket for constant access; the other holds the rest of the trip's materials and stays inside the waterproof pack liner.
- Replace the "today" bag every ~5 days on long trips — the plastic scratches up and reads worse.
- **For constant heavy rain**, step up to a commercial plastic map case (Sea to Summit, Aquapac). Not worth it for typical 3-season weather — short storms, dries fast.
- **A wet map is not a catastrophe.** Laser toner doesn't bleed like printer ink; dries out fine. Print with laser, not inkjet, for this reason.
- **Don't carry maps loose in a pocket.** They fold badly, tear, and get lost. Ziploc or map case only.

**Picks**

- *Ziploc Gallon Freezer Bag* — Skurka's pick and the template default.
- *Sea to Summit TPU Guide Map Case* — commercial alternative for wet conditions.
- *Aquapac Medium Map Case* — heavier-duty wet-weather case.

## Writing instrument

**Principles**

- **Basic black or blue medium ballpoint pen.** Not gel (smudges), not pencil (fades), not felt-tip (runs when wet).
- Map margins are the default journal — "the geographic context adds color to my notations." Carry a few sheets of blank paper if you're a prolific writer.
- **Below freezing:** ink pens freeze. Keep a spare in a jacket pocket and swap when the writing pen quits.

**Picks**

- *Pilot G2* — template pick. Common, reliable, under $2.
- *Uni-Ball Jetstream* — smooth ballpoint alternative.
- *Rite in the Rain pen and paper* — for trips where writing in rain actually matters.

## What sources stay quiet on

Sat messengers (SPOT, inReach, Zoleo) — treated separately in `tools-first-aid.md`. Paper route descriptions printed as separate pages vs. annotated onto maps — Skurka mentions both but doesn't prescribe. Beacons and avalanche-specific navigation — winter-only, out of 3-season scope.
