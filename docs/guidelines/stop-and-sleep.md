# Stop & Sleep — Insulated Clothing and Dedicated Sleeping Layers

The "Stop" and "Sleep" tiers of Skurka's four-layer clothing system: puffy jacket, optional puffy pants, and dedicated sleeping clothes for wet climates. Source: Skurka Ch. 4 Items 10–13; Clelland Tips 21, 48, 49, 50, 61; *Lighten Up!* clothing and sleep-system chapters.

**Core principle: Stop and Sleep are one warmth budget, not two.** All three sources insist the insulated jacket is part of the sleep system — you wear it at night, which lets you carry a lighter bag. Sizing the puffy and the bag independently is a mistake. Clelland's dial is explicit: lighter puffy → heavier quilt, or heavier puffy → lighter quilt. Both trade-offs work.

**No redundancy.** One hiking shirt, one puffy, one pair of underwear. Redundancy lives in dedicated sleeping clothes (when wet climate forces it), not in duplicated Go Suit items. Wash the one pair; don't carry a second.

---

## Insulated jacket (puffy)

**Principles**

- A puffy, not a fleece. Puffies are ~3× warmer than synthetic fleece per weight (synthetic fill) and ~5× warmer (down fill). Fleece is still needed as a *mid-layer* in sustained wet (see `element-protection.md`) but it is not the primary camp/sleep insulation. "The heavyweight fleece jacket is for traditional camping" (Clelland).
- **Down over synthetic** when you can reliably keep it dry. Down wins on warmth/weight, compressibility, and lifespan (years of use with no notable decline). Synthetic loses loft after a long trip and has to be replaced.
- The "synthetic is warm when wet" claim is largely a straw man — insulated outerwear is not rain gear, should never get soaked, and cold-water-soaked synthetic doesn't keep you warm either. The real down-vs-synthetic dividing line is *ambient humidity and trapped perspiration over multi-day trips*, and modern water-resistant down treatments (DownTek, DriDown, Down Defender) narrow even that gap.
- **Sizing for 3-season** (Skurka's calibration): ~2.5 oz of 800-fill down for the humid East (hoodless); ~3.0–3.5 oz of 800-fill down for the Mountain West (hooded). Go up from there only if you sleep cold, expect colder nights, or plan to push into the shoulder season.
- **Cut:** slightly oversized to layer over a hiking shirt + mid-layer (and ideally to squeeze a rain shell inside when desperately cold). Mid-butt length with an hourglass cut so it doesn't bunch at the waist when cinching. Thumb loops help. Pants should rise near the belly button so there's no draft gap at the waist.
- **Avoid features that add weight without warmth:** fleece-lined pockets, heavy zippers, stretchy underarm or cuff panels, over-engineered cinch cords. 10- to 20-denier shell fabric is ideal; heavier fabric is unnecessary and WP/B fabric actively hurts (poor breathability wets the insulation from inside).
- **Keeping it dry is technique, not fabric.** Waterproof pack liner, periodic sun-drying (Skurka: "the first sunny meadow I see, I lay it out for 20 minutes"), well-ventilated shelter, separate dry-bag for the down items on wet trips.
- **Stupid-light warning:** the puffy is also the camp-morale item. Underspec'ing it to save an ounce is a classic bad call — the jacket is worn for hours every evening and often all night.

**Picks**

- *Mountain Hardwear Ghost Whisperer* (~8 oz, 800-fill) — Skurka's benchmark reference. Expensive (~$350) but the shell, insulation, and construction represent the mainstream premium.
- *Montbell Plasma / Superior Down* — lighter and cheaper comparable option from the same category.
- *Patagonia Down Sweater Hoody* — template default, more features and durability than the ultralight picks but warmer per dollar.
- *Rab / Western Mountaineering* down jackets — similar tier to MHW, for users who want alternatives.
- *Enlightened Equipment Torrid Apex Jacket* — synthetic fallback when sustained wet makes down a bad bet; Apex insulation, handles moisture well.
- *Costco / Uniqlo / Target* house-brand down jackets — Skurka explicitly says these are worth checking; the premium brands use the same Asian factories and similar fills.

## Insulated pants

**Principles**

- **Conditional, not default.** Skurka adds puffy pants only when nighttime lows are below ~30°F or when he expects long camps below ~40°F. Clelland treats them as optional "especially if you have a really wimpy quilt" (max 8 oz). *Lighten Up!* doesn't treat puffy pants as a standard 3-season item at all.
- The 3-season answer for cold legs at rest stops is usually: thermal long-john bottom under hiking pants, plus rain pants over everything if it's windy. That stack traps enough heat for most stops without adding a dedicated insulated pant.
- If you do carry them, size over hiking pants; the zippers-down-the-leg versions are only worth it if you'll also use them over winter boots.

**Picks**

- *Montbell Superior Down Pants* — Skurka's 3-season pick. ~8 oz, pull-on style, "leaves little room for improvement" for pure 3-season use.
- *Enlightened Equipment Torrid Apex Pants* — synthetic alternative; pair with the Torrid jacket for a wet-climate set.
- *Military surplus M-65 insulated pants (long version)* — under $20, legitimately functional budget option.

## Underwear B / Bra B (rotation)

**Principles**

- **Sources barely address this.** Skurka wears one pair and washes it; Clelland explicitly says "men do not need a second pair." The only direct endorsement of a second pair is Clelland's concession that "women might find that exercise and change of environment will influence their monthly cycle; extra undies might be helpful — no more than three total."
- None of the three books endorse a dedicated rotation sports bra.
- **Practical guidance:** if you bring a second pair, treat it as a hand-washed rotation (same protocol as hiking socks A/B), not as a sleeping-specific item. Wash pair A during the day, wear pair B; swap overnight.
- If this row in the template is blank on a given trip list, that's consistent with the source guidance.

**Picks**

- No source-endorsed picks at this rotation slot. Use the same model as Underwear A / Bra A from `worn.md`.

## Sleeping top

**Principles**

- A wet-climate decision, not a universal item. Skurka's clearest rule: on semiarid-West trips he doesn't carry one; in wet climates (Appalachians, PNW, Alaska) skipping it is "stupid light" — "a one-pound weight savings, but what is the cost to your body and your morale of a crappy night of sleep?"
- **Job description:** guarantee a dry night of rest when hiking clothes haven't dried. This means (a) the sleeping top lives in the pack during the day and never gets worn while hiking, and (b) if hiking clothes are still wet in the morning, you change *back* into the wet hiking clothes rather than putting the sleeping layer at risk.
- **Fabric:** chosen by expected nighttime low. Mild nights → lightweight short-sleeve synthetic. Cold nights → expedition-weight base layer or 100-weight fleece pile.
- *Lighten Up!*'s alternative: a long-sleeve zip-neck synthetic hiking shirt doubles as a pajama top, with no dedicated sleeping layer. This works only when the hiking shirt is reliably dry at bedtime — fine in dry climates, risky in sustained wet.
- Not for keeping the bag clean or for food-scent management. "Frequent backcountry laundering and good personal hygiene address the first concern, and I'm dubious of the effectiveness of the second" (Skurka).

**Picks**

- Any *polyester base-layer top* — no brand loyalty needed; the spec is "dry, clean, comfortable to sleep in."
- *100-weight fleece pullover* — Skurka's cold-weather sleep-top default when a lightweight base layer isn't enough.

## Sleeping bottoms

**Principles**

- Same wet-climate logic as the sleeping top. On dry trips, skip them and sleep in hiking pants or underwear. On wet trips, they are part of the same kit as the sleeping top and share the same discipline (no daytime wear, never sacrificed to hiking).
- Clelland uses "long undies" as a universal sleeping bottom, even in dry climates, because his sleep system counts on the puffy + long undies + long undies stack for warmth. This is a valid alternative to Skurka's "bring them only for wet trips" rule, and it folds naturally into the "wear everything at night" approach.
- *Lighten Up!*'s approach: a thermal long-john bottom doubles as the cold-leg-at-rest layer and the sleeping bottom — one pair, two jobs.

**Picks**

- Any *synthetic or merino long-john bottom* — Patagonia Capilene, Smartwool 250, etc. Same picks serve both the cold-legs role in `element-protection.md` and the sleeping-bottom role here.
- *100-weight fleece pile pants* (Polartec Power Stretch, Melanzana, etc.) — Skurka's escalation for cold sleeping conditions.

## Wet-climate sleep-clothing discipline

When sleeping clothes are in the kit, protect them like rain gear protects a down jacket — they only work if they stay dry.

1. Store them inside the pack, inside a waterproof dry bag or the pack liner, separate from the hiking clothes.
2. Never wear them during the day, even "just for a minute."
3. Never wear them to cook in (spark/grease/stink).
4. In the morning, put wet hiking clothes back on. The first hour of hiking warms the body fast enough to make this bearable, and the alternative is compromising the one dry system you have.

## What sources stay quiet on

Dedicated sleeping sports bras, women's-specific wet-climate rotation patterns, and lightweight kid-specific insulation setups aren't addressed. For Underwear B / Bra B in particular, the honest answer is "sources don't support a strong default" — the row often stays blank.
