# Tools, First Aid, Emergency, & Utility

The miscellany drawer: headlamp, knife, first-aid kit, repair kit, sat comm, firestarter, bug net, bear spray, and the trip-specific add-ons. Source coverage is uneven here — Skurka, Clelland, and *Lighten Up!* scatter this material across multiple chapters rather than treating it as a single category. This file is the condensed synthesis of what they do say, with explicit notes on what they stay silent on.

**Core principle (all three books): multi-use beats single-purpose.** A headlamp that doubles as a fire-starter reading light, a knife that handles every cutting task, a bandana that filters coffee and doubles as a stuff sack. Don't stack single-purpose gadgets.

**Core principle: "just in case" is the wrong frame.** Clelland Tip 20 is explicit — replace "just in case" with specific condition-driven decisions. "Just in case it snows" becomes "snow is in the forecast, so I'm bringing..." If you can't state the condition, you don't need the item.

## Light (headlamp)

**Principles**

- **A headlamp is near-essential.** Hands-free light for camp, pre-dawn starts, late-afternoon route-finding, middle-of-night bio breaks, foot care. Skurka and Clelland both carry one.
- **Output target:** 100–250 lumens for general camp and pre-dawn hiking. More is fine but adds battery weight. Most UL headlamps hit this range at sub-2 oz.
- **Red-light mode** preserves night vision and keeps you from blinding tentmates. Worth having.
- **Rechargeable USB vs. AAA:** USB-rechargeable is lighter, topsup from a power bank, no battery disposal. AAA is longer-proven and serviceable when lithium cells fail. Pick based on trip length and your charging infrastructure.
- **Backup light:** on trips where a headlamp failure would be dangerous (night hiking, solo, alpine), carry a tiny keychain light or a backup cell. Overkill for short summer trips.

**Picks**

- *Nitecore NU25* — template default; ~1.7 oz, 360 lumens, USB-C rechargeable, red mode. Long-running template pick.
- *Black Diamond Spot* — AAA alternative, heavier but proven.
- *Petzl Bindi* — ultralight, 200 lumens, rechargeable.
- *Petzl e+Lite* — 1 oz emergency backup.

## Sleep mask

**Principles**

- **Situational.** Critical for Arctic summer (24-hour daylight), full moon with clear sky in open country, and trips where tarps or bivies leave the face exposed. Skip on most East Coast forest trips.
- A Buff pulled over the eyes works as a double-duty sleep mask — one less dedicated item. This is the universal multi-use move when you don't carry a dedicated mask.

**Picks**

- *Buff Original* — doubles with the insulated-headwear slot in `element-protection.md`.
- *Bucky 40 Blinks* or similar — dedicated sleep mask for hikers who can't sleep in a Buff.

## Knife

**Principles**

- **Small blade plus scissors is plenty for 3-season backpacking.** "This is not Survivor" — Skurka. Most backpacking cutting tasks are food bags, tape, cordage, and first-aid moleskin — a dedicated survival knife is dead weight.
- **Scissors are more useful than a second blade** for most jobs (Leukotape, bandage trimming, cordage, nail trimming).
- **Don't skip a blade entirely** — foot care, food prep, gear repair, and emergency shoe repair all need one.
- Multi-use bonus: a Swiss Army knife with small scissors is a classic UL compromise. No need for pliers, saws, or toothpicks.

**Picks**

- *Victorinox Classic SD* — 0.7 oz, small blade, tiny scissors, nail file. Template default.
- *Opinel No. 6* — slim folding blade; Skurka's template example.
- *Derma-Safe Razor* — lightest "knife" option at ~0.1 oz; folding razor blade, adequate for foot care and food bags. Backpacking minimalist pick.

## First aid kit

**Principles**

- **Build it yourself; commercial kits are full of stuff you don't need.** Skurka's approach: medications, wound care, tweezers, anti-chafing, foot care — all decanted into a small Ziploc or pill bags.
- **Scope by trip and party:** solo short trips need less than long guided trips. Guides and trip leaders carry more; individuals within a group carry a lean personal kit.
- **Contents that all three books or their broader context support:**
  - Meds: ibuprofen, acetaminophen, Benadryl, antihistamine, Imodium, personal prescriptions.
  - Wound care: a few bandages, a few gauze pads, medical tape.
  - Tools: small tweezers (ticks and splinters), small scissors (often combined with the knife).
  - Foot care: Leukotape P (12-inch strips on mailing-label paper, Skurka's storage method), blister pads, antibiotic ointment.
  - Alcohol wipes for cleaning wounds and tools.
  - A few safety pins.
- **Decant medications** into small pill bags labeled with sharpie. Don't carry the retail bottle.
- **The kit is safety gear.** *Lighten Up!*: classifies the first-aid kit among items that return to "pile one" (always packed) regardless of whether the last trip used them.
- **Training outweighs gear.** A Wilderness First Aid (WFA) or Wilderness First Responder (WFR) course is worth more than every gadget you could cram into the kit.

**Picks**

- *DIY kit* — the consensus approach. Build to the trip's risk profile, not to a catalog.
- *Adventure Medical Kits Ultralight/Watertight .5 or .7* — commercial baseline if you don't want to build from scratch. Remove items you don't need.

## Foot care kit

**Principles**

- **Distinct from the first-aid kit** because foot problems are the most common trip-ender and you use this kit far more often than the FAK.
- **Core contents** (from `footwear.md` and Skurka's chapter):
  - **Leukotape P** — zinc-oxide rayon-backed tape. Apply at first hot spot; sticks for days through sweat and water. Precut 12-inch strips on mailing-label paper to save space.
  - **Hydropel** or similar petroleum-based ointment — apply under the foot morning and night in sustained-wet conditions to prevent maceration.
  - **Blister pads / moleskin** — for already-formed blisters. Moleskin is boot-era gear and doesn't stick well to UL shoes; Leukotape is the better primary.
  - **Antibiotic ointment** — for popped or broken-skin blisters.
  - **Nail clippers** — optional; small clippers if your nails need it during the trip.
- **Prevention beats treatment.** Good shoe fit, good socks, sock rotation, early hot-spot response — see `footwear.md`.

**Picks**

- *Leukotape P* — core taping material.
- *Hydropel* or *Squirrel's Nut Butter* — petroleum-based skin protection in sustained-wet conditions.
- *Joshua Tree Climbing Salve* — general-purpose skin balm; works for hands and feet.
- *Compeed / Spenco 2nd Skin* — blister pads if Leukotape isn't enough.

## Repair kit

**Principles**

- **Build for the specific failure modes of your gear:** shoe blowouts, pad punctures, zipper failures, shell tears, stove issues.
- **Core contents** (Skurka's list, decanted):
  - **Tenacious Tape** (or similar) for shell and tent fabric repair.
  - **Aquaseal** (or Seam Grip) for shoe, tent, and gear-seam repair.
  - **Duct tape** — re-rolled onto a pencil stub or credit card to save bulk.
  - **Glue dots or Krazy Glue** small tubes.
  - **A needle and strong thread** (or dental floss from the hygiene kit).
  - **Spare cord** (a few feet of the guyline cord).
  - **Safety pins** — often multi-tasked from the first-aid kit.
- Skurka folds the repair kit and first-aid kit into one small bag for short trips; splits them for longer ones.
- **Don't carry a full sewing kit** — a needle and one spool of thread covers everything you'll realistically fix in the field.

**Picks**

- *Tenacious Tape* — fabric repair; 1-2 sq inches is usually enough.
- *Gear Aid Aquaseal* — small tube.
- *Duct tape* — rerolled.
- *Sail needle + spool of Kevlar thread* — heavy-duty thread for shoe repair.

## Sat comm

**Principles**

- **Satellite communicators** (two-way messaging + SOS) are the modern emergency kit for remote trips and solo hikers. Skurka doesn't cover them in depth in the 2017 book edition; the category has moved fast since.
- **Use cases:** emergency SOS, two-way messaging with home, weather forecasts on long routes, check-ins for solo travel.
- **Tradeoffs:** monthly or per-message subscription, battery life, weight (4–8 oz), and the risk of dependency — a sat comm is not a substitute for judgment and skill.
- **Handheld GPS with integrated sat comm** (Garmin inReach Explorer+, GPSMAP 66i) consolidates navigation and communication if you were going to carry a handheld anyway. See `navigation.md`.
- **Cell phone only in areas with reliable reception.** Don't count on it in the backcountry for safety.

**Picks**

- *Garmin inReach Mini 2* — template default sat comm. ~3.5 oz, two-way messaging, SOS.
- *Zoleo Satellite Communicator* — less expensive alternative with similar feature set.
- *Garmin inReach Explorer+ / GPSMAP 66i* — handheld GPS + sat comm combo.
- *Spot X* — older category entrant, less capable.

## Firestarter

**Principles**

- **A real fire-starting capability matters in emergency situations** — warmth, drying gear, morale, signaling. Even if you don't plan campfires, you should be able to make one.
- **Minimum kit:** a Bic lighter (your stove ignition, see `kitchen.md`) plus a tinder source that works wet. Clelland uses discarded Mylar food wrappers. Skurka carries fire starter in his "accessory pouch."
- **Dedicated tinder options:** cotton balls in petroleum jelly (pack in a small pill bag), commercial tinder tabs, Esbit cubes (pull double duty with the kitchen).
- **Ferro rods and magnesium sparkers** are backup-only. They need a stable surface for accurate sparks, which stoves don't provide (Skurka dismisses them for stove use). For fire starting, they're slower and less reliable than a Bic.
- **Waterproof matches and commercial fire starters** are overkill for 3-season but legitimate on shoulder-season and winter trips.

**Picks**

- *Bic Mini* — primary ignition; see `kitchen.md`.
- *Vaseline-soaked cotton balls* — lightweight tinder, ~0.1 oz for 3-4 pieces.
- *Esbit cube* — doubles as emergency stove fuel and fire starter.
- *UCO Stormproof Matches* — backup for long or cold trips.

## Power (portable charger)

**Principles**

- **Match the charger to your electronics load** — phone, headlamp, watch, camera, sat comm. Most 3-season trips need 5,000–10,000 mAh to comfortably run a smartphone (airplane mode) + headlamp for a week.
- Skurka uses an Anker PowerCore+ mini for mid-trip charges on photo-heavy trips or extended phone-as-GPS use.
- **Solar panels** are compelling only on very long unsupported trips; they're heavy, fragile, and demand direct sun that backpackers on wooded or valley trails don't reliably get.
- **Cables count.** Carry the minimum — a short USB-C or Lightning cable, not the full desk setup. Bundle with the charger in a small pouch.

**Picks**

- *Anker PowerCore 10000* — template default; 10,000 mAh, ~6 oz.
- *Nitecore NB10000* — carbon fiber shell, lighter at ~5 oz.
- *Anker PowerCore+ mini* — Skurka's small-trip pick, ~3,350 mAh.

## Insect repellent

**Principles**

- **Picaridin over DEET for gear compatibility.** DEET dissolves plastics, synthetic fabrics, and some pack materials. Picaridin is as effective and won't eat your gear.
- **Permethrin on clothing** (not skin) is the multi-day answer for bug country. Treat shirts, pants, and socks before the trip; lasts ~6 weeks or 6 washes. Factory permethrin-treated garments from Insect Shield or ExOfficio BugsAway are equivalent.
- **Head net** is the single most effective bug defense piece — see `worn.md` and below.
- **Decant** repellent into a 1-oz dropper or squirt bottle; a trip doesn't need a full retail bottle.

**Picks**

- *Sawyer Picaridin 20%* — Skurka's recommended 3-season repellent.
- *Sawyer Permethrin* — clothing/gear treatment; pre-trip spray.
- *Ben's 100 DEET* — for extreme bug country only, and keep it off synthetic gear.

## Bandana

**Principles**

- **High multi-use value:** pot grip, washcloth, coffee filter, neck cooling, sun drape, emergency water pre-filter, tourniquet, bag closure, map-bag lining. Clelland explicitly cites the bandana as a classic multi-use item.
- **Cotton is fine here** — one of the few places cotton earns its place, because you use it wet more often than not and the absorbency is the feature.
- **Size:** 18-22" square is the standard. Don't oversize — it's just cotton and you can trim.

**Picks**

- *Any cotton bandana* — $2 at an outdoor or army surplus store. Template default.
- *Buff Original* — polyester alternative that doubles as neck gaiter, beanie, sleep mask, headband. Different tradeoffs; less absorbent but dries fast.

## Head net

**Principles**

- **Near-mandatory in bug country.** Lightweight, tiny packed size, transformative when bugs are bad. See `worn.md` for the full treatment.
- **Mosquito mesh, not no-see-um mesh** — no-see-um mesh is suffocating and stifles airflow. Use only where no-see-ums are the actual threat.
- **Wear it over a brimmed hat** so the mesh doesn't touch the face.

**Picks**

- *Sea to Summit Mosquito Head Net* — longer neck seal, template default.
- *Coghlan's Head Net* — cheap but too short at the neck.

## Bear spray

**Principles**

- **Carry in grizzly country, period.** Non-negotiable for Glacier, Yellowstone, the Northern Rockies, interior Alaska. Black-bear-only country (most of the lower 48) is a judgment call — bear spray is still effective but the bear pressure is usually lower.
- **Hipbelt holster, always.** Never buried in the pack, never clipped to the back of the pack, never in a wand pocket. If a bear surprises you, you have ~2 seconds — the spray must be in your hand in that time. Clelland is emphatic.
- **Versus firearm:** lighter, no legal complexity, more effective in actual bear encounters per study data. Both Skurka and Clelland endorse spray over firearms for backpacking.
- **One can per hiker** (or at minimum, per two hikers in a tight group). Don't share a can across a long, spread-out party.
- **Don't test it.** Test firings are for training sessions, not trailheads. And don't store it in a hot car trunk; extreme heat can cause canister failure.

**Picks**

- *Counter Assault Bear Deterrent Spray* — template default, widely endorsed.
- *Frontiersman Bear Spray* — equivalent alternative.

## Sit pad

**Principles**

- **Situational luxury.** Keeps butt dry and warm on cold, wet, or rocky ground. Most backpackers can do without; some trips reward it.
- **Double-duty:** cut from a worn-out closed-cell sleeping pad. A Z-Seat folding pad is the commercial version.
- Skip entirely on trips where you'll sit only at dry camp.

**Picks**

- *Therm-a-Rest Z-Seat* — 2 oz folding closed-cell sit pad. Template default.
- *DIY from old sleeping pad scrap* — free and lighter.

## Group shelter (optional)

**Principles**

- **A large flat tarp for group cook/lounge space** earns its weight on group trips in wet climates (Appalachians, Olympics, Alaska). Skurka: "about one extra pound per group, avoids everyone retreating to cramped personal shelters." See `shelter.md` for tarp shape discussion.
- Solo and couple trips don't need this.

**Picks**

- *Rab Guides Siltarp 2* (8×10 ft) — Skurka's group tarp pick.
- *Aqua Quest Safari Tarp* — template alternative.

## Foot traction (crampons / microspikes)

**Principles**

- **Not 3-season gear by default.** Carry only when the specific trip has icy trails, frozen snowfields, or steep hard snow in the forecast. Deferred to an alpine override.
- **Microspikes** are the lightweight answer for non-technical icy trails — shoulder-season river crossings, early-season snowfields, frozen morning trails. Not crampons; different use cases.

**Picks**

- *Kahtoola MICROspikes* — template default. ~11 oz, fits over trail runners.

## Ice axe

**Principles**

- **Not 3-season gear.** Carry only when the trip requires self-arrest, step-chopping, or steep snow travel. Deferred to an alpine/winter override.
- Training matters — an ice axe you can't use is a weight liability.

**Picks**

- *Black Diamond Raven* — template default straight-shaft mountaineering axe.

## What sources stay quiet on

Specific sub-items with thin or no coverage in Skurka, Clelland, or *Lighten Up!*: knife-model specifics beyond "small and simple," commercial first-aid kit reviews, specific sat-comm brands (the category post-dated the 2017 edition of Skurka), solar charger specifics, e-readers, music players (actively discouraged by Clelland). For any of these, fall back to trip conditions and the general multi-use/needs-first principle above.
