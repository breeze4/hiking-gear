# Hydration — Bottles, Treatment

Water carriage and purification. Source: Skurka "Water" chapter; Clelland Tips 101–106, 112; *Lighten Up!* Part 4 "Water."

**Core principle: carry as little as possible between sources.** Water is ~2 lb/qt — one of the heaviest consumables. The UL strategy is to drink at sources ("camel up") and carry the minimum to the next one, not to over-provision with a 3 L bladder by default. Clelland, paraphrasing Ryan Jordan: "if you arrive at a water source with water still on your back, you have made a mistake."

**Hydration-quality gauge:** urine color. Clear or slightly yellow-tinged = good; dark yellow = drink more. Skurka runs slightly over-hydrated ("clear and copious"), drinking 1–2 qt in the first hour of the morning. Watch for hyponatremia too — dumping plain water in hot conditions without electrolytes can leave you "tipsy" and is a real edge case on desert trips.

**Trip-planning math:** most hikers drink ~4 qt/day in normal 3-season conditions. Arid and hot conditions can push to 1 qt/hour. Plan water sources ahead on the map before the trip; don't discover them on trail.

## Bottles (primary)

**Principles**

- **Lightweight collapsible or disposable plastic bottles, always.** A 1-L Smartwater bottle is 0.8 oz; a 34-oz Platypus SoftBottle is 0.9 oz; a 1-qt Nalgene Lexan is 6.3 oz. That difference is 5+ ounces per bottle, multiplied by however many you carry. Nalgenes are for car camping and around-town use, not backpacking.
- **Carry two bottles, not one big one.** Smaller bottles are easier to hold at a shallow fill point, easier to pass through a cone filter, and the second one is redundancy against puncture or loss. It also matches the "sipper + pack reserve" rhythm most hikers fall into on trail.
- **Smartwater 1 L is the thru-hiker default** and works with Sawyer Squeeze threads out of the box. Gatorade bottles are tougher; any disposable drink bottle from the recycle bin is legitimate. Replace when the plastic starts to stress-crack.
- **Hydration bladders (hose + bite valve) are actively rejected** by both Skurka and Clelland. Refill requires pack-off / unthread / remove / refill / rethread; remaining volume is invisible; the full bladder bulges against your back; mouthpieces leak; tubes freeze in cold; closures are miserable with mittens. "WANTS, not NEEDS" — Clelland.
- **Fill technique:** a cooking mug or a Ziploc works as a scoop for shallow or narrow sources, and lets you inspect for floaties before pouring into the bottle. For trickles, walk the source until you find the clearest drip.

**Picks**

- *glacéau Smartwater 1 L* — Skurka's and Clelland's baseline disposable. Free from the recycling bin, durable for weeks of trail use, threads fit Sawyer Squeeze.
- *Platypus SoftBottle 34 oz* — Skurka's collapsible pick, 0.9 oz each. Prefer the standard Closure Cap over HyperFlow (less leaky, faster flow).
- *Gatorade-style disposable* — thicker wall (~1.5 oz) for hikers who puncture Smartwaters.
- *CNOC Vecto 2L* — collapsible with a roll-top and a hose port, popular as a dirty-water bag for Sawyer setups and as a secondary storage bottle.

## Bottles (storage / dry-camp capacity)

**Principles**

- **Baseline dry-camp rule:** always carry at least 2 qt of *capacity*, even when water is abundant, so you can dry-camp — 1 qt for dinner, 1 qt to start hydrating in the morning.
- **Scaling up for arid trips:** add one or more 2 L collapsibles and fill them only at the last reliable source before a dry stretch. *Lighten Up!* pushes this to 4 qt total capacity for desert/dry-camp trips — "not because you'll always fill it, but because you need the option."
- Collapsibles are the right shape for storage — they take no space when empty and conform to the pack when full.
- **Center heavy water loads** in the pack against the back, or split across side pockets for balance. Don't put 3 lb of water in one side pocket and nothing on the other side — your center of gravity shifts.
- **Group trips:** add a 4 L reservoir for meal prep and camp breakdown (Skurka uses an MSR Dromedary Bag). One big bag beats four small ones for the group kitchen.

**Picks**

- *CNOC Vecto 2L or 3L* — template default for dry-camp storage. Roll-top is faster to fill from trickles than a narrow-mouth bottle.
- *Platypus Platy Bottle 70 oz (2 L)* — Skurka's dry-camp pick. $13, ~1.2 oz empty.
- *Platypus Big Zip EVO 3 L* — larger-volume collapsible.
- *MSR Dromedary Bag 4 L* — Skurka's group reservoir. Nylon-reinforced, durable, heavier (~7 oz).

## Treatment (primary)

**Principles**

- **All three sources recommend chlorine dioxide (Aquamira) as the 3-season primary treatment** for solo and small-group backpacking. Kills protozoa including *Cryptosporidium*, bacteria, and viruses; minimal taste; tiny weight; works without effort.
- **Dwell times:** 15 min for most pathogens, 30 min for turbid water, 4 hours for *Cryptosporidium* on cold water. Plan the dose so there's enough lead time before you drink — do it at the fill point, hike on, sip after 15+ minutes.
- **Iodine is obsolete.** Cheap and light, but ineffective against *Cryptosporidium*, tastes terrible, contraindicated for pregnancy and thyroid patients. All three books flag it as outdated. Don't use it as a primary.
- **The Aquamira premix trick** (Skurka + Clelland):
  1. Decant A and B into small labeled dropper bottles, and a third bottle labeled "Mix" (opaque or dark).
  2. In the morning, mix equal drops of A and B into the Mix bottle (e.g., 20 + 20 for a small bottle of premix).
  3. Carry the premix bottle in a pocket for fast use. Dip, drop, and go — no waiting.
  4. Color test: the mix should be bright yellow. If it has gone clear, it's lost potency — dump and re-mix.
  5. Longevity: ~24 h hot/sunny, ~48 h cool, never more than 3 days. When in doubt, dump.
  6. McNett (the maker) officially discourages premixing on potency grounds. Skurka and Clelland have used it for years with education and judgment. Know what you're doing before you adopt it.
- **Dosage is a judgment call.** The label says 7 drops A + 7 drops B per liter (14 drops of premix). Clelland scales down for trusted sources and up for sketchy ones.
- **Below ~15°F**, drops freeze — switch to chlorine dioxide tablets. Tablets also carry EPA "purifier" certification; drops are marketed as "odor-causing bacteria" treatment as a labeling artifact.
- **Source assessment matters.** "Assess the source" is a real skill, not an excuse to skip treatment. Both Skurka and Clelland admit to drinking untreated water from low-risk alpine sources (small springs, snowmelt outflows, remote lakes) but carry treatment for everything else. *Lighten Up!* takes the firmer "assume all water is contaminated" stance.

**Source-assessment heuristics**

- Short distance from the origin and low upstream use = better.
- High volume dilutes contamination.
- Clear water is lower risk than turbid water (floaties shield pathogens from chemicals and UV).
- Moving water is lower risk than stagnant.
- Lush moss/vegetation = good; algae bloom = bad.
- No campsites, mines, grazing, or carcasses upstream.
- **Is there a dead elk in the stream?** (Clelland's actual checklist item.)

**Picks**

- *Aquamira Water Treatment Drops* — Skurka's and Clelland's long-term pick. $15, ~3 oz kit. Decant into smaller dropper bottles for pack weight.
- *Aquatabs / Potable Aqua Chlorine Dioxide tablets* — cold-weather alternative when drops freeze; also EPA-certified as purifiers.
- *Sawyer Squeeze* ($40, ~3 oz) — the solo gold standard for fast on-trail filtering. Mechanical filter, 0.1 micron absolute. Fits Smartwater threads. Can pair with Aquamira for a "highly suspect water" combination. Backflush regularly to maintain flow rate; don't let it freeze (kills the filter).

## Treatment (secondary / group / backup)

**Principles**

- **Gravity filters are the group / camp / large-volume answer.** No-effort flow, high throughput, let the gravity do the work while you set up camp. Platypus GravityWorks is the reference pick.
- **Sawyer Squeeze + Aquamira is the solo "gold standard" combo** — Squeeze for fast small-batch filtering on the move, Aquamira for overnight bulk treatment in a dry camp or for sketchy sources. Skurka calls this "the ultimate purifier combination."
- **Pre-filter turbid water** before chemicals or UV — a bandana, a coffee filter, or a small plastic kitchen funnel does the job. Keep sediment out of the filter housing, strip the "floaties" that shield pathogens.
- **UV (SteriPEN) is treated skeptically** by all three books. Fast (~90 seconds per quart) and effective in clear water, but unreliable: narrow-mouth bottle incompatibility, useless in turbid water, battery failure modes, electronics vulnerable to drops and wet. If you use a SteriPEN, carry a backup treatment.
- **Boiling works** and is free when the stove is already running for a meal. Otherwise wastes fuel and time. Not a primary strategy.
- **Pump filters are Skurka's least preferred** — heavy (12–16 oz), slow (1 qt/min), tiring. Skip for 3-season backpacking; use gravity or squeeze instead.
- **Filter + chemical combo** for the sketchiest water (trampled cattle puddles, beaver ponds, downstream of a popular camp): filter first for particles and large pathogens, then chemicals for the smallest survivors.

**Picks**

- *Platypus GravityWorks 4 L* — Skurka's group / camp pick. $120, ~12 oz. Dirty bag, clean bag, fill, hang, wait. 2 qt/min.
- *Sawyer Squeeze* — as primary or secondary depending on the trip; covered above.
- *MSR Guardian* — purifier that also handles viruses and chemicals; $350, 17 oz. Overkill for North American 3-season, relevant for international travel or highly suspect water.
- *Aquamira tablets* — backup to any filter-based primary, and cold-weather fallback for Aquamira drops.

## What sources stay quiet on

Electrolyte supplementation (Clelland mentions it briefly; Skurka notes hyponatremia as a real desert risk but doesn't recommend a specific product), reusable water-test strips, and vacuum-insulated bottles (Skurka notes the Hydro Flask as "convenient, almost luxurious" but not a 3-season pick). Fall back on trip conditions and the general discipline above.
