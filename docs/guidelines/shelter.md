# Shelter — Tarp, Tent, Bivy, Hammock, Stakes, Guylines

Rainfly, inner nest, ground cloth, poles, guylines, stakes. Source: Skurka shelter chapter; Clelland Tips 90, 91, 94, 95; *Lighten Up!* shelter chapter.

**Core principle: pick by needs, not by category.** Start with the environmental hazards you actually expect — rain, wind, bugs, ground moisture, hostile critters — and the campsite types you'll hit. Then pick the lightest shelter that genuinely meets those needs. Decide on storm-worthiness, campsite compatibility, adaptability, condensation resistance, and living space *before* you shop a product category.

**Shelter is not a warmth layer.** A few ounces of down gives more warmth than the same weight of fabric and poles. Sealing a shelter for warmth creates condensation. If you're cold, add to the sleep system; don't overbuild the shelter.

**Condensation is the single biggest UL-shelter gotcha.** Single-wall UL shelters breathe less than a double-wall tent's fly+inner pair. Mitigations: ventilation (open sides, vents), site selection (avoid valleys and streamsides in dead calm), keep the fabric taut, wipe down with a pack towel, accept a small weight penalty for a bivy.

## Fabric cheat sheet

- **Silnylon** — the workhorse UL coated fabric. Strong, waterproof (3,000+ mm HH at 1.5 oz/sq yd), stretches when wet (re-tension guylines at rain onset and before bed). Seams need silicone-glued sealing, not tape. Highly flammable — never near flame. Life: ~300–500 hard-use nights.
- **Dyneema Composite Fabric (DCF, formerly Cuben)** — premier fabric. Lighter, no water absorption, no stretch (pitches taut and stays taut). Expensive, cottage-only, shorter life at the lightest weights (~250 nights at 0.75 oz). Worth it if you're optimizing at the margin; not worth it if you still have lower-hanging fruit elsewhere in the kit.
- **PU/PE-coated nylon** — weaker and less waterproof than silnylon at equal weight, but factory-tapeable seams and meets fire-code standards.
- **No-see-um mesh** — smaller holes than mosquito mesh, less airflow, stifles ventilation on calm nights. Use only where no-see-ums are actually present.
- **WP/B membranes** in shelter fabric — only in mountaineering single-walls and bivies. Wrong choice for 3-season: heavy, pricey, less breathable than coated fabric + bug mesh.

## Rainfly or tarp (the main shelter body)

**Principles**

- Most 3-season backpackers are served by one of: a single-wall trekking-pole tent, a double-wall tent, a tarp + bug component pair, or (in wooded terrain) a hammock system.
- **Double-wall tents** (practical ~2–3 lb) are complete protection with generous living space. Condensation forms on the fly, not on the inner, so occupants stay dry. Popular and foolproof; the weight is the cost.
- **Single-wall tents** (trekking-pole-supported, cottage-made) are lighter and faster to pitch but condensation-prone; bring a pack towel in humid conditions. Minimally adaptable (one shape, fixed geometry).
- **Tarps** are a sheet of waterproof fabric paired with a ground cloth, bivy, or bug nest to form a complete system. Same protection as a tent, usually lighter (1–2 lb total), more adaptable, less condensation-prone, roomier. Cost: more skill required, and you feel exposed the first few nights.
  - **Flats** — lightest per coverage area, most versatile pitches. Good for group cook/lounge space.
  - **A-frames** — catenary ridgeline, fixed shape, pitches tighter than a flat and flaps less. Go big at the corners rather than rigging beaks.
  - **Mids (pyramid)** — most storm-worthy tarp shape. Symmetric profile, steep walls shed rain and snow, single trekking pole support. Door must close in rain/snow, killing ventilation when humidity peaks — that's the trade.
- **Trekking-pole-supported shelters are the UL default** — but only if the poles are adjustable. If your poles aren't adjustable, buy a shelter that doesn't depend on them.
- **Poncho-tarp** is a weak compromise. After 5,000+ miles of testing it, Skurka rejected it: poor shelter, flaps as rain gear, soaks you making camp in a storm. Skip.
- **Hammocks** are underused where good ground sites are rare (AT, eastern woods, Appalachians, Rocky Mountain NP, Glacier NP designated sites). Legit answer when ground sites are lumpy, sloped, wet, overgrown, or highly impacted. Weight penalty, steep learning curve, requires trees; incompatible with existing ground sleep kit.

**Picks**

- *Durston X-Mid* (1P or 2P) — common template default; trekking-pole-supported, semi-double-wall, rectangular footprint, excellent ventilation. Not in the source books (post-2017) but fits the "mid" category and is widely adopted.
- *Mountain Laurel Designs SoloMid XL* — Skurka's mid pick. Trekking-pole-supported single-wall mid.
- *Mountain Laurel Designs Grace Tarp* + *MLD Superlight Bivy* — Skurka's mild-conditions Western setup (~18 oz total).
- *Sierra Designs High Route 1 FL* — Skurka co-designed; 2 lb 3 oz, double-wall, trekking-pole-supported with offset poles so they don't split the sleeping area. A reasonable "one-tent for everything" 3-season pick.
- *MSR Hubba Hubba NX* or equivalent mainstream double-wall — template default for backpackers who don't want to learn tarp pitching. 3 lb+, but foolproof.
- *Tarptent / Six Moon Designs / ZPacks* single-wall cottage tents — lighter alternatives for the "tent, not tarp" user.
- *Warbonnet Ridgerunner* bridge hammock — Skurka's 3-season pick for eastern woods; pair with underquilt and A-frame tarp.

## Nest, bivy, or hammock insert

**Principles**

- Under a tarp you need some combination of ground cloth, bivy, and/or bug mesh to complete the shelter. Pick by bug pressure and ground moisture.
- **Full bug nest** (freestanding or tarp-hung): most interior space; best for peak bug season. Usually model-specific to a given tarp.
- **Bug bivy**: tube of bug mesh + waterproof floor. Lighter than a nest, no usable living space, bugs can bite through mesh where it touches skin — pair with a hat or loose clothing.
- **Water-resistant bivy sack** (DWR top, small no-see-um panel at the head): eliminates the ground cloth, blocks spindrift and splatter, adds a few degrees of warmth by reducing convective loss, provides some bug protection, and makes cowboy camping actually viable. Very popular with flat/A-frame users.
- **Mountaineering WP/B bivies** — skip for 3-season. Stifling in humidity, condensation-prone, inner sweat soaks gear, no usable living space when pinned down.
- In mild conditions with light bug pressure, a head net + hat + hooded wind shirt + snug quilt can replace the bug component entirely. Saves pounds.

**Picks**

- *MLD Superlight Bivy* — Skurka's pick; DWR top, small mesh head panel, ~8 oz.
- *Mountain Laurel Designs Innernet* — full bug nest for MLD mids. Template pick for bug-heavy trips.
- *Six Moon Designs Serenity NetTent* — most-interior-space nest for a flat/A-frame system.
- *Outdoor Research Bug Bivy* — lighter than a nest, minimal living space.

## Ground cloth or footprint

**Principles**

- Protects the shelter floor (or the sleeper, in floorless setups) from abrasion and moisture. Don't overbuy: the lightest option is also the most effective.
- **Polycryo** (window insulation film) is the ultralight sweet spot. Surprisingly durable, fully waterproof, $5, ~2 oz trimmed to shape. Replaceable every few trips.
- **Tyvek** (house wrap) is heavier, tougher, slightly noisy, cheap. A legitimate alternative if you like the durability.
- **Footprints** (shelter-specific, coated nylon, sized to the tent outline) are heavier and pricier than a generic ground cloth without any performance gain. Skip them unless the tent is designed to use one for floor protection.
- **Bathtub floors** help on wet ground, but the need for one suggests site selection failed.
- In floorless shelters, stack two ground cloths on wet nights — it's a few ounces for a real difference.

**Picks**

- *Gossamer Gear Polycryo Ground Cloth* — Skurka's pick. $5, ~2 oz, trimmable with scissors.
- *Tyvek* cut to size — template default; heavier but more durable.

## Poles

**Principles**

- **Trekking poles are the default shelter pole** for tarp and single-wall UL tents. Saves the weight of dedicated aluminum tent poles.
- Dedicated poles (carbon or aluminum) are still the right answer for freestanding double-wall tents and for hikers who don't use trekking poles.
- For mids supporting heavy snow load, a single stout shaft (or a dedicated carbon pole) is better than strapped trekking poles.

**Picks**

- *Trekking poles* — see `worn.md` picks (Black Diamond Alpine Carbon Cork, etc.). Any adjustable lever-lock pair works for shelter support.

## Guylines

**Principles**

- Nonfreestanding shelters need guylines to achieve shape and stability. Freestanding tents pitch better with them too — roomier, better ventilated, more storm-worthy.
- **Cord length:** 3–8 ft per tie-out, bowlined to the tie-out point. Longer for apex tie-outs (more distance to the anchor), shorter for side tie-outs.
- **Tensioning system:** for low tie-outs, run the cord to the anchor and back through the bowline loop, then lock with a slippery half-hitch pulled back toward the anchor. This gives a 3:1 mechanical advantage — you can tension a silnylon shelter with very little force. For apex tie-outs, a trucker's hitch with a slipknot-made bight does the same job.
- **Silnylon stretches when wet.** Always re-tension at rain onset and before bed. Failure to re-tension is the most common UL-shelter user error — sagging fabric puts condensation in contact with the sleeping system.
- **Why this system:** secure, adjustable, quickly learned, flexible anchor location (important on rocky or rooty ground), no fixed knots or plastic tensioners to snare on brush.
- **Winter variant:** *Nite Ize Figure 9 Small* rope tighteners in place of the slippery half-hitch, so you can cinch with mittens on.

**Picks**

- *Mountain Laurel Designs LiteLine* 1.5 mm (~$16/60 ft, 0.5 g/ft) — Skurka's lightest pick. Stiffer and rougher than thicker cord, but saves ~1.5 oz across six guylines.
- *PMI 3 mm Utility Cord* (~$5/50 ft, 2.3 g/ft) — Skurka's workhorse pick; easier to handle and more durable. Better for group trips and practice shelters.
- *Kelty Triptease* (~0.8 g/ft) — Spectra core with reflective nylon sheath; easier to handle than LiteLine, reflective for night visibility.

## Stakes

**Principles**

- **Aluminum Y- or V-stakes are the 3-season default.** Tough, good holding power in most ground, drivable into rocky soil without bending.
- **Titanium skewers** are lighter (~0.2 oz vs. 0.5 oz) but have about half the holding power and bend easily. Limit them to noncritical side tie-outs; never on A-frame apex or mid corner tie-outs.
- **Avoid plastic stakes** — they snap when the wind picks up, which is exactly when you need them.
- **Stake holding power depends on ground, angle, and pull direction, not just stake design.** Drive at a 15–20° angle away from the load; pile a rock on top for a big holding bump.
- **Anchor != stake.** Skurka has used sticks, trees, downed logs, roots, buried snowshoes, buried skis. A guyline loop around a root is often stronger than any stake.
- **Snow:** skip stakes; use buried skis, snowshoes, ski poles, or branch deadmen you can leave behind.
- **Ridgeline stakes need to be stout.** Clelland carries two longer, stouter stakes specifically for the nose/tail of his tarp ridge and uses lighter stakes for side tie-outs.
- **Transport:** carry sharp stakes in a small fabric sack or Tyvek envelope so they don't puncture the sleeping pad.

**Picks**

- *MSR Groundhog 7.5"* — template default; aluminum Y-stake, excellent all-around holding. Fits Skurka's "aluminum Y" category exactly.
- *Kelty Y-Stakes* (~0.5 oz) — Skurka's budget pick.
- *MSR Mini Groundhog* — shorter Groundhog, better in shallow alpine soil; stake of choice for non-load-bearing tie-outs.
- *Gossamer Gear Tite-Lite* (6", 0.2 oz titanium skewer) — Skurka's gram-counting pick; only for side tie-outs, not the ridge.
- *Easton aluminum nail stakes* — Clelland's ridgeline stake.

## Pitching discipline

A shelter is a skill, not a purchase. Pitch any new shelter in a backyard or low-risk environment **before** you trust it to a storm. Practice the guyline tension system until it's automatic. Clelland: "I use any excuse to cowboy camp" — on clear nights the tarp is a pillow. Look at the sky; pitch only when you need to.

If the weather is uncertain, scout a tarp-ready spot before dark and stash a stake or two; pitching by headlamp in the middle of the night is easier when you've pre-picked the site.
